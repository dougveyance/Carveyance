HR-Vs often live in cities, with tight parking, curbs and short trips, and plenty were bought as a first new car. Neither is a reason to avoid one, but check the bumpers, wheels and tires as carefully as the engine, and confirm the recalls by VIN.

## First, know which one it is

Decode the VIN to confirm the year, trim and whether it has all-wheel drive. A 2016–2022 HR-V has the 1.8-liter and the Magic Seat; front-drive LX and EX models from 2016–2018 may have the 6-speed manual. A 2023 or later HR-V has the 2.0-liter, a CVT and no Magic Seat. Honda Sensing is standard on the 2019–2022 EX and above.

## The test drive

Start from cold and listen for rattles. Accelerate gently and then hard: the CVT should feel smooth, with no shudder, slipping or flare in engine speed. On a manual, check that the clutch bites cleanly and doesn't slip in a high gear. On a 2023–2025 car, turn the wheel slowly from lock to lock in a parking lot and note any heavy or sticky spots, a symptom of the steering gearbox recall. On a 2019–2022 car, start it several times and make sure the rearview camera appears each time. On an all-wheel-drive car, make a few tight, slow turns and listen for groaning.

## Cabin and body checks

On a 2016–2022 car, flip the Magic Seat cushions up and fold the backs down, and check that every latch locks. Look at the bumpers, the lower doors and the wheels for parking damage, the tires for uneven wear, and under the cargo floor for water. Ask for the service records, including the CVT fluid.

## Recalls: check by VIN

- **2016 and 2018–2019:** 18V777. A brake caliper problem, fixed by the dealer.
- **2018–2023:** 23V858, plus 20V314 (2018–2019) and 21V215 (2019). The fuel pump may fail.
- **2019–2022:** 23V046 and 24V384. The rearview camera image may not display when the car is started with a key. Cars fixed under the first recall may need the second.
- **2019–2022:** 26V332 (2019–2021) and 24V064 (2020–2022). The front passenger seat weight sensor can fail, so the passenger air bag may not work correctly.
- **2023–2024:** 23V782. A front seat belt pretensioner may be missing a rivet.
- **2023–2025:** 24V744. Friction inside the steering gearbox can make steering difficult.
- **2024:** 24V859 and 26V054. Driver's seat fasteners or the seat cushion frame may be faulty.
- **Every year:** run the VIN at [NHTSA's recall lookup](https://www.nhtsa.gov/recalls) and ask for proof that each repair was done.

## Walk away if

The car has a branded title, flood damage or a musty smell you can't explain. Walk away from a CVT that shudders or slips, a steering recall the seller hasn't had done on a 2023–2025 car, and from any seller who won't allow an independent inspection.

## What is it worth?

We don't publish average prices. They go stale quickly, and an average that mixes a 2017 LX with a 2025 EX-L tells you very little. Compare 20 to 30 listings for the same generation, trim, drive and mileage, with the same title history, and keep dealer asking prices separate from what cars actually sold for. [FitDrive's deal check](/fitdrive/) does the first part for you. Cross-shop a Mazda CX-30, Subaru Crosstrek or Toyota Corolla Cross of the same age, and take the cost of tires and brakes off the price.

## Our shortlist

The HR-V we'd buy depends on what goes in the back. For most people, a 2023 or later Sport with its steering recall done is the better car to drive every day. For anyone who hauls bikes, plants or flat-pack furniture, a 2019–2022 EX with the Magic Seat is the more useful one, and it costs less.

- **Best all-rounder:** a 2023 or later Sport or EX-L.
- **Best value:** a 2019–2022 EX.
- **Most useful cargo space:** any 2016–2022 HR-V.
- **Cheapest way in:** a 2017–2018 LX, manual or CVT.
