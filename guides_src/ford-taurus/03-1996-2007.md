The 1996 Taurus tried to repeat the 1986 revolution and found out lightning doesn't strike twice. The oval styling — windows, dash, even the rear glass — unsettled exactly the loyal buyers Ford could not afford to lose; the car stayed America's best-seller in 1996 only because 51 percent went to rental fleets, and in 1997 the Camry took the title, 397,156 to 357,162. A GL started at $18,600, about $39,900 in 2026 dollars. The 2000 refresh squared the shapes off and briefly rallied — 382,035 sales in 2000 — but Ford had stopped investing, and the slide to fleet-only sales ended with the Atlanta line stopping on October 27, 2006, the final car going to Chick-fil-A founder S. Truett Cathy.

## The buying logic

Nobody cross-shops these; you buy one because it's $2,500 and runs. Within that logic there are right answers. The **Duratec 24-valve** (200 hp) is the engine to find — modern, smooth and durable — while the 155-hp **Vulcan** is slower but famously immortal; after 2003 the Vulcan was all that remained. Transmissions are the watch item either way: the AX4N wants fluid history and shows its tiredness as flare between gears. The oval cars are cheaper than the 2000s cars for pure styling reasons, which makes a well-kept 1996–99 Duratec the stealth value — and the wagon, dead after 2005, the quirky keeper.

## What to check

Rust underneath on northern cars, coolant history on 3.8-adjacent anything (the Essex left after 1995, but inspect any V6 for head-gasket weep), transmission behavior warm, and completed recalls by VIN. Interior wear is the honest odometer on fleet refugees — a 90,000-mile ex-rental often looks worse than a 150,000-mile grandma car, and the grandma car is the buy.
