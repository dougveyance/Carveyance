These cars attract people who like to drive. That's the whole appeal, and it's also the risk. Many have done autocross or track days, worn aftermarket parts or made a few enthusiastic launches. None of that is automatically bad, but each one raises the bar for the records you should ask to see.

## The years at a glance

| Model years | What they are | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| 2017–2020 | Toyota 86, formerly the Scion FR-S. 2.0-liter, 205 hp manual, 200 hp automatic | Fuel pump recall on 2018–2019 cars (21V-587); an ignition key recall on some early 2017 automatics (16V-920) |
| 2021 | No US model | Toyota skipped the year between generations |
| 2022 | First GR86. 2.4-liter, 228 hp | Rear turn signal recall (23V-609); early reports of oil starvation from excess factory sealant |
| 2023–2026 | The same 2.4-liter car | Track use, modifications and engine history |

## Confirm the generation

Toyota 86s in this guide are 2017–2020 cars. The GR86 starts with the 2022 model year. Check the model year and engine from the VIN, because the registration year and the listing title can blur the difference. Confirm the transmission, any performance packages and any accident repairs.

## Look for hard-use evidence

Check the wheel fitment, the tire brands and their date codes, uneven tread wear, the strut towers, the panels underneath, the jacking points, signs of brake overheating and any non-standard suspension parts. Ask about modifications and any engine tuning or oil-system work. On a manual, feel how the clutch takes up and how the gears go in. On an automatic, check the response and the service history. Then have it inspected by a shop that knows the platform.

## The tires tell a story

A matched set of good tires in the right sizes is good evidence that the owner cared about how the car drives. Worn inner shoulders, a lot of negative camber and rub marks can point to a track alignment or poorly thought-out modifications. Neither track use nor aftermarket suspension means abuse on its own, but either one raises the standard for the service records you should ask for. Check the brake rotors, the brake fluid history and whether the rear differential fluid has been changed.

## The oil question, in proportion

The GR86's engine has a reputation, and it's worth understanding exactly what it is.

Some early 2022 cars lost their engines to oil starvation after excess sealant applied at the factory broke loose and clogged the oil pickup. In one widely shared case, Toyota at first refused to cover the engine because the car had been on a track, then replaced it. Separately, owners' own instrumented testing has shown oil pressure dropping during long, hard corners on track. Subaru, which builds the engine, says it stands behind the design and that the engine is built for road use. A class action covering 2017–2023 cars has also been filed.

None of that means a road-driven GR86 is going to fail. It means you should ask about track days, oil-warning lights and any engine work, and have a specialist in the Subaru and Toyota platform look at the oil and the records before you accept a seller's explanation. Toyota has said that driving events it provides won't void the warranty, apart from damage caused by the track use itself. If regular track driving is part of your plan, read Toyota's guidance and budget for track-specific maintenance, separately from normal commuting service.

## Recalls: check by VIN

- **2017:** 16V-920. On some early 2017 automatics with a key ignition, the key could be removed without the transmission being in Park, so the car could roll away. Dealers inspect and correct the wiring.
- **2018–2019:** 21V-587. The low-pressure fuel pump inside the tank can fail and stall the engine. Dealers replace the pump.
- **2022:** 23V-609. The rear turn signals can stop working intermittently. About 5,700 GR86s are included, and dealers replace both rear lamp assemblies.
- **Every year:** run the VIN at [NHTSA's recall lookup](https://www.nhtsa.gov/recalls) and ask for proof that each repair was done. Recalls and service bulletins aren't the same thing, so ask about both.

## What a good deal looks like

Treat originality, service history and condition as the core of the value. A cheaper car with mismatched tires, an aggressive tune and patchy records can end up far more expensive than a clean one. We don't publish average prices. They go stale quickly and hide the differences that matter. Compare listings for the same year, generation, transmission, mileage and title history, and get an insurance quote before you commit. [FitDrive's deal check](/fitdrive/) helps with the comparison.

Buying from a private seller, or keeping the car past its factory warranty? Service plan providers to compare include [Chaiz](https://www.chaiz.com), [Endurance](https://www.endurancewarranty.com) and [Olive](https://www.olive.com). Carveyance doesn't rank or recommend them. Read what each one covers and excludes, especially for track use and modified cars, and see the [FTC's guidance on auto service contracts](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2023/03/what-know-about-auto-service-contracts-extended-warranty-scams) first.

## Avoid buying a fantasy

The 86's charm comes from involvement, not silence, comfort or torque. Spend enough time in one to know whether the noise, the low roof and the small trunk are livable. A used manual may be the best enthusiast buy, but an automatic can suit someone whose driving is mostly in town. The sensible deal is the version you'll actually enjoy driving, with enough left in the budget for tires and some instruction.

## Our shortlist

- **Best value:** a stock 2017–2020 86 manual with records and a matched set of tires.
- **Best overall:** a GR86 with a clean history, every recall closed and no unexplained engine work.
- **Commuter:** an automatic of either generation.
- **Track car:** one with documented track maintenance, or a budget to start it.
