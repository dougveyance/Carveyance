Can a $28,350 Electric Truck Replace Your Generator?

Ford says its cheapest EV will be able to run a house in a blackout, with home hardware that costs a fraction of what the F-150 Lightning needed. How vehicle-to-home power works, what it costs against a generator and a home battery, and what Ford still hasn't said.

By Nathaniel Ruiz · 7 minute read

The lights go out, the refrigerator goes quiet and the street goes dark. In the driveway sits a pickup with a battery several times bigger than a home battery, and unless it's an F-150 Lightning with about $5,000 of extra hardware on the garage wall, that battery can do nothing about it.

Ford wants to change that with the Fathom, the electric midsize pickup it will start selling late next year from $28,350. On October 27 it explained how the truck will power a house: more than 10 kW of household AC power, Ford projects, with home hardware that it expects to cost far less than the system it sold for the F-150 Lightning. "Cost has been the biggest obstacle," wrote Kay Hart, the general manager of Ford's EV business, Model e. The idea isn't new. The price is.

How a truck powers a house

A car battery stores direct current. A house runs on alternating current. Something has to convert one to the other, and something has to make sure that when the truck is feeding the house, none of that power runs back into the grid, where it could injure a line worker repairing the outage. Those 2 jobs are what make vehicle-to-home power expensive.

The F-150 Lightning did the conversion outside the truck. Its 80-amp Ford Charge Station Pro cost $1,310, and the Home Integration System that made backup possible, an inverter, a transfer switch and a small battery to restart the system, was sold through Sunrun for $3,895, both before installation. That's $5,205 in 2022 prices, about $6,000 in today's money, and installed systems ran far higher. The Fathom moves the inverter into the truck, in the module Ford calls the e-box, so the truck itself puts out AC. What's left on the wall is simpler: a Home Power Station, which Ford expects to cost about half as much as its first charger, and, for outages, a Home Backup Power Panel at least 60% cheaper than the first-generation backup hardware.

Ford's percentages work out to about $650 for the charger and about $2,100 for the full backup kit, before installation, figures TechCrunch reported. The system is designed to meet the UL 1741 and IEEE 1547 standards that utilities use for solar panels and home batteries, and Ford has set up a working group with 5 utilities, including Pacific Gas and Electric and Exelon, because most interconnection processes "were written for rooftop solar and stationary battery storage," Ford told CBT News.

Ford didn't invent vehicle-to-home power. It's trying to make it cheap enough to be ordinary.

How long the lights stay on

This is the number Ford hasn't given, because it hasn't said how big the Fathom's battery is. The Lightning's extended-range battery holds 131 kWh, and Ford said it could run an average house, using about 30 kWh a day, for 3 days, or up to 10 with rationing. Sam Abuelsamid of GreenCars estimates the smaller Fathom battery at 50 to 60 kWh, which would mean about 1.5 days for a typical house. That's an outside estimate, not Ford's, but the shape of the answer is clear: a Fathom would get most households through an ordinary outage, not a week-long one, unless it can drive somewhere to recharge.

The catch: the truck has to be home

A generator in the backyard is always there. A home battery on the garage wall is always there. A pickup is only there when nobody has driven it to work, and a truck that powered the house all night starts the next day with less range. That's the real trade-off, and it's why the comparison isn't simply cost per kilowatt-hour. A standby generator on a gas line can run for days. A Powerwall pairs naturally with rooftop solar and refills every sunny morning. The Fathom's strength is that the backup comes free with something you were buying anyway, and that its battery is much bigger than a typical home battery.

The same hardware can also work when the power is on. Ford's Home Power Management feature would charge the truck when electricity is cheap and run the house from it when it's expensive, with the utility's approval and an active Ford plan. TechCrunch notes that payments for joining a utility's demand program can reach $1,000 a year in some places, though Ford hasn't promised any.

What Ford still has to say

Almost everything that would let you do the math is still missing: the battery size, the range, the prices of the home hardware and what installation costs, which brought the Lightning's system to about $6,000 to nearly $20,000 in total, by GreenCars' estimate. "The launch remains on track for fourth quarter next year," Jim Farley, the chief executive, said on October 28, and the starting price of $28,350 is only a start. "We'll have a range that goes to the high $30,000 range," Andrew Frick, who runs both Ford Blue and Model e, said of the trim levels on the same call. Preorders open early next year.

Ford isn't the only company offering this. The 2027 Chevrolet Bolt, which also starts under $30,000, can power a house through GM's own home-energy hardware. But TechCrunch calls the Fathom the cheapest American EV that will be able to power a house, and if the home hardware really costs what Ford's percentages suggest, it turns backup power from a luxury add-on into something an ordinary household might actually buy.

The short version

The Ford Fathom, due late in 2027 from $28,350, will be able to send more than 10 kW to a house, Ford projects. Because its inverter is built in, the home hardware should cost a fraction of the F-150 Lightning's $5,205 kit: about $2,100, by Ford's percentages. It would get a typical house through a day or so without power, by one outside estimate, as long as the truck is in the driveway. Ford still hasn't given the battery size, range or hardware prices.
