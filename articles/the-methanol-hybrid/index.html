The Hybrid That Runs on Methanol, Gasoline or Anything in Between

Geely's new Xingyao 6 EF is a plug-in hybrid with 1 fuel tank and no fixed recipe: pure methanol, pure gasoline or any blend of the 2. How an engine copes with that, why methanol cuts the car's range, and why its climate case depends on where the fuel comes from.

By Theo Wren · 8 minute read

Most of the car industry is arguing about 3 answers: batteries, hybrids and gasoline range extenders. On November 3, in the northern Chinese city of Shenyang, Geely launched a car built around a fourth. The Galaxy Xingyao 6 EF is a plug-in hybrid sedan whose engine runs on methanol, a liquid alcohol that China makes in enormous quantities, on gasoline, or on any mix of the 2 poured into the same tank.

Geely calls it "the world's first production methanol hybrid passenger vehicle." That's generous: Geely itself launched a methanol hybrid Emgrand in 2022. But the new car is a plug-in hybrid, sold as a family car, from 76,800 yuan (about $11,500) for early orders. More than 6,000 are already working as taxis, ride-hailing cars and official cars, Geely says.

How 1 engine burns 2 fuels

Methanol and gasoline burn very differently, so an engine that takes either, in any proportion, has to know what's in the tank. Geely's answer is a sensor in the fuel system that reads the methanol concentration in real time, from changes in an electrical current. The engine computer then adjusts how much fuel it injects and when it fires the spark plugs. "M100" means pure methanol, "M15" means 15% methanol in gasoline, and this car accepts anything from M0, ordinary gasoline, to M100.

The rest of the engine is built around methanol. It injects fuel at 350 bar, uses high-energy ignition and methanol-specific iridium spark plugs, and Geely says it starts on pure methanol at temperatures near −40°C (−40°F) without warming up on gasoline first. It claims a thermal efficiency of 48.15%, meaning that share of the fuel's energy becomes work, which it calls "the highest among mass-produced methanol engines globally." Methanol is also corrosive to some materials, so Geely says it uses methanol-resistant parts, dedicated additives and lubricants, and extra protection around the front subframe, battery and high-pressure fuel tank.

Why methanol goes less far

The catch is energy. A gallon of methanol holds 57,250 Btu, according to the US Department of Energy, against about 112,000 to 116,000 Btu for gasoline. In the DOE's own words, "1 gallon of methanol contains 50% of the energy" of a gallon of gasoline. So with a full tank and a full battery, the car goes about 1,250 km on methanol against 1,880 km on gasoline.

The gap on the road is smaller than the chemistry suggests. With its battery depleted, the car uses 6.7 liters of methanol per 100 km on China's test. Working back from Geely's gasoline range, it uses roughly 4.2 liters of gasoline, by our calculation, so it needs about 60% more methanol than gasoline, not twice as much. That suggests the engine gets more work from each unit of methanol's energy than from gasoline's. And methanol is cheap in China: at about 2.98 yuan (about 44 cents) a liter, Chinese reports put the running cost at about 20 yuan (about $3) per 100 km.

Where the methanol comes from

This is the part that decides whether a methanol car is clean. Methanol can be made from almost anything with carbon in it, and in China it's mostly made from coal: about two-thirds, according to a 2017 estimate from the US Energy Information Administration. Made that way, it's far dirtier than gasoline over its whole life.

Methanol from coal produces about 2.8 times the emissions of gasoline per unit of energy, well to wheels. Made from captured carbon dioxide and hydrogen from renewable electricity, so-called green methanol, it can cut emissions by "up to 95%," according to the Methanol Institute, an industry group. Geely has invested in both ends of that range. In 2015, it put $45.5 million (about $64.7 million today) into Carbon Recycling International, the Icelandic company whose George Olah plant in Iceland has made methanol since 2012 from the carbon dioxide of a geothermal power plant and hydrogen made by splitting water. And it backed the plant in Anyang, Henan province, billed as the world's first commercial-scale CO2-to-methanol plant, which can make 110,000 metric tons a year. The Anyang plant's hydrogen comes from coke-oven gas, a byproduct of making coke for steel mills, so it's lower-carbon methanol rather than truly green.

Is it safe?

Methanol is poisonous. Britain's Health Security Agency says as little as 4 to 10 mL swallowed can cause permanent blindness in adults, and it can also be absorbed through the skin or inhaled. China has tested methanol cars at scale, though. In a government pilot from 2012 to 2018, 1,024 methanol vehicles, 904 of them Geely taxis, covered more than 184 million km, and health checks on 1,199 workers found no problems, according to the Methanol Institute. China has national standards for methanol fuel and for its safe use in vehicles.

Where it makes sense

A methanol car needs methanol stations, and they're rare. Geely says China has more than 1,400 of various kinds, 1,228 of them in its own network, with a target of 4,000 by the end of 2027; the Methanol Institute counted about 500 in 2025. China has more than 100,000 gasoline stations. In Guiyang, where methanol taxis have run for years, more than 90% of the taxi fleet ran on methanol by 2023, Geely says. With an engine that starts on methanol in deep cold and a range of more than 1,000 km, the car makes the most sense where batteries struggle, in cold regions and on long drives, as a complement to Geely's EVs and ordinary hybrids rather than a replacement for them.

Outside China, the methanol network barely exists, and there's no sign the car is coming to America. But the idea is worth understanding. A plug-in hybrid that can run on a liquid fuel made from captured carbon is one more way to cut emissions without asking every driver to charge a big battery. Whether it does cut them comes down to one question no sticker can answer: where the methanol in the tank was made.

The short version

Geely's Xingyao 6 EF is a plug-in hybrid whose engine runs on methanol, gasoline or any mix, using a sensor that reads the fuel and adjusts the engine to match. Methanol holds half the energy of gasoline, so with a full battery the car goes about 1,250 km on a tank of it against 1,880 km on gasoline, though it's cheap to run in China. Its climate case depends entirely on the fuel: methanol made from coal produces about 2.8 times the emissions of gasoline, while green methanol can cut them by up to 95%.
