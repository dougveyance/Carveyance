The hybrid chapter of the Fusion story starts with a trophy and a correction. The 2010 Fusion Hybrid — 191 hp combined, 41 mpg city — was good enough to win North American Car of the Year outright, borrowing the battery-durability credibility its Escape Hybrid cousin had already earned in taxi fleets. Then the 2013 redesign arrived claiming 47 mpg across the board, the press and owners couldn't reproduce it, and Ford revised the rating to 42 combined in the 2013–14 mileage controversy. The revised number is the honest one — and it's still excellent.

## What they're like to own

The second-generation Hybrid (188 hp combined) returns a real-world low-40s commute and drives like a normal, quiet Fusion; the eCVT has proven durable, and pack failures are rare enough to be forum news rather than a pattern. The **Energi** plug-in adds a bigger battery for 22 miles of electric range (25 from 2019) and 97 MPGe — a short-commute car that touches gasoline only on road trips, at 42 mpg when it does. Its trunk pays the price for the battery, which is the main practical check on a test drive.

## The buying math

Hybrids and Energis were expensive new — a 2017 Energi Platinum listed at $39,120, about $53,700 in 2026 dollars — and depreciated like everything else wearing a Fusion badge, which is the entire opportunity. Used, they typically cost a small premium over equivalent gas cars and repay it in fuel within a couple of years of normal driving. Shop them like any aging hybrid: a full warm-up test drive watching the battery gauge cycle, service records over mileage, and on the Energi, confirmation that the charge port and included cord actually work. A healthy one is the cheapest cost-per-mile Fusion there is.
