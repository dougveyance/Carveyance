What 5 Years Do to an EV Battery

Ford says the first Mustang Mach-Es kept 92% of their battery capacity, on average, after about 5 years with their owners. How lithium-ion batteries age, what that number means in miles, and why 5-year-old Mach-Es have still lost about 60% of their value.

By Priya Raghavan · 9 minute read

Ask anyone shopping for a used EV what worries them, and the battery comes first. It's the most expensive part of the car, you can't check it with a flashlight, and everyone has heard that it wears out. On November 9, Ford put a number on that worry. Across more than 36,000 Mustang Mach-Es from model year 2021, the car's first, the average battery had kept 92% of its usable capacity after an average ownership period of 5.2 years.

That's a good number with an awkward companion. According to iSeeCars, a 5-year-old Mach-E has lost 60.8% of its value, more than the typical EV and far more than the typical car. If the batteries are mostly fine, something else is dragging the price down.

What Ford found

The figures come from Ford's own connected-vehicle data, as of August 2026, written up by Kevin Vander Laan, Ford's technical leader for high-voltage battery cells and systems. The cars have covered 2 billion miles, about 55,600 each by our calculation, on NCM (nickel-cobalt-manganese) cells. "The number I look at closely is the spread," Vander Laan wrote, and the spread is tight: 95% of the cars sit between 87% and 97%. More than 2,000 owners have passed 100,000 miles "on their original battery," averaging better than 91%, and more than 30 have passed 200,000 miles at an average of 88%. That matches the 12% loss Tesla reported for the Model S and X at 200,000 miles in its 2022 Impact Report; its 2023 report put the Model 3 and Y Long Range at 15%. "So here we are with a five-year report card, and it looks great," Vander Laan told InsideEVs.

Why batteries fade at all

A lithium-ion battery ages in 2 ways at once. Cycle aging comes from charging and discharging; calendar aging comes from time alone, even with the car parked. Much of the loss happens in a thin film on the anode, the solid electrolyte interphase, which protects the cell but grows by trapping lithium that would otherwise carry charge. The thicker it gets, the slower it grows, so a peer-reviewed model led by the US National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) found that calendar fade "tracks with square-root-of-time." In Recurrent's words, "the initial drop in capacity is clear, after which the degradation slows."

Recurrent names 3 accelerants: extreme temperatures, fast charging and sitting at a high state of charge. The NREL-led model shows aging speeding up exponentially as cells get hotter, and heat is worst paired with a full charge. "The worst situation is keeping a fully charged battery at elevated temperatures," says Battery University. Ordinary driving may be kinder than lab tests suggest. In a Nature Energy paper in December 2024, Stanford researchers found that driving-like discharge patterns gave cells up to 38% more equivalent full cycles, and that for private owners, "time becomes the predominant cause of aging over cycling," in the words of the lead author, Alexis Geslin.

What fast charging costs

Geotab, a fleet-telematics company, tracks fade on real cars. Its 2026 study of more than 22,700 EVs found average fade of 2.3% a year, up from 1.8% in its 2024 analysis, and it pointed to charging habits. "What has changed is that charging behavior now plays a much bigger role in how quickly batteries age," Geotab's Charlotte Argue told Gizmodo in January. Hot climates added about 0.4 points a year.

Losing 8 points in 5.2 years works out to about 1.5 points a year on a straight line, the same as Geotab's lightly fast-charged cars. That's consistent with Mach-Es that mostly charge at home, though Ford didn't break its data down by charging habits.

What 92% means in miles

Ford reported capacity, not range, but scaling the original EPA ratings by battery health gives a fair estimate, the same method InsideEVs used. The 2021 Mach-E started at $43,995 for the Select (about $54,700 in today's money) and $61,000 for the GT (about $75,900).

On the 300-mile Extended Range rear-drive car, Ford's 87–97% band means roughly 261 to 291 miles. The warranty floor is far off: Ford of Canada's 2021 warranty guide covers the pack if capacity falls below 70% of where it began, within 8 years or 160,000 km (about 99,400 miles), and Recurrent lists the US terms as 8 years or 100,000 miles. The 92% average sits 22 points above that line.

So why are the cars worth so little?

iSeeCars studied more than 950,000 used cars, all 5 years old, sold between March 2025 and February 2026, with original prices adjusted to 2026 dollars. "Electric vehicles continue to lose more value than any other segment," said Karl Brauer, its executive analyst. "That high upfront cost comes back to haunt electric cars on the used market."

Set that beside Ford's data and the 2 don't connect. A car that has kept 92% of its battery hasn't lost 61% of its usefulness. The loss more plausibly traces to what these cars cost new and to what new EVs cost now. Electrek reports that the 2027 Mach-E starts at $39,995 plus a $2,045 destination charge, with an 88 kWh battery as standard. Even the $42,040 total is less than the $43,995 a 2021 Select cost with destination, before adjusting for inflation, and every used Mach-E is priced against it.

The used market has also turned this year. Cox Automotive put the average used-EV listing at $36,767 in September, up 6.4% from a year earlier, and Recurrent found used-EV prices 8% higher than a year ago: "Used cars don't normally appreciate, so that got our attention." The 60.8% describes cars bought at 2021 prices and sold before most of this year's rise. For this cohort, the value loss doesn't appear to be explained by battery fade.

What Ford didn't say

This is Ford's own data, on Ford's own site, with no independent audit or peer review cited, and Kelley Blue Book noted that the methodology wasn't provided. Ford doesn't say how it calculates state of health, or whether replaced packs are in the average; only the 100,000-mile group is described as being on its original battery. There's no breakdown by battery, climate or charging habits, and nothing on the 5% of cars outside the band. Method matters: Recurrent has noted that several automakers use software changes to keep displayed range at 100% for 5 years. Its own data, reported last November, puts pack replacements outside big recalls at about 2% for second-generation EVs such as the early Bolt EV and Model 3 (2017–2021 models, by InsideEVs' count) and 0.3% for 2022 and later, but neither Recurrent nor Ford has published a Mach-E figure.

If you own one, or want one

Ford's advice is short: "Charge on Level 2 at home for daily driving and save DC fast charging for the days you need it." It also suggests: "Precondition while you're still plugged in and park in the shade in the summer if you have the option." As Vander Laan put it, "Owners should not have to become battery chemistry experts to take good care of their electric vehicles."

For used buyers, the battery in a 2021 Mach-E is unlikely to be the weak point, and the depreciation that hurt first owners is the opening for second ones. Still, the average hides the 1 car in 20 outside Ford's band, so ask for a battery-health report, and remember that past 100,000 miles a car is likely outside the US battery warranty. The first Mach-Es aged the way good batteries are supposed to. Their prices aged like expensive new cars.

The short version

Ford's data on more than 36,000 model-year 2021 Mach-Es shows 92% average battery health after an average ownership period of 5.2 years. That fits how batteries age: fast at first, then slower, and faster with heat, a high charge and heavy fast charging. Yet 5-year-old Mach-Es have lost 60.8% of their value, so battery fade doesn't appear to explain the depreciation. The caveat: it's Ford's own study, without a published method or replacement data.
