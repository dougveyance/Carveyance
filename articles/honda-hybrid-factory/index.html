Honda Wrote Off Its EV Plan. Now It's Building a $3 Billion Hybrid Factory.

In 1 week, Honda announced a new Ohio plant, booked ¥484.8 billion (about $3.1 billion) of EV-related losses in a single quarter, raised its forecast anyway and made the next CR-V hybrid-only. The real lesson isn't the hybrid. It's a factory that can change its mind.

By Nathaniel Ruiz · 8 minute read

Honda's week read like a confession and a business plan stapled together. On Monday, November 9, it said it will spend $3 billion on a new car plant in Ohio. On Tuesday, it reported ¥484.8 billion (about $3.1 billion) of EV-related losses for July to September, most of it money set aside to compensate suppliers for electric models it canceled, and raised its full-year profit forecast in the same breath. On Thursday, it said the next CR-V will be sold only as a hybrid.

Read quickly, that's a company changing religions: out with batteries, in with hybrids. That's the wrong lesson, and Honda's own announcement says why. The new plant isn't just a hybrid plant. It's a plant that can build hybrids, gasoline cars or EVs, and Honda hasn't named a single model for it. After paying dearly for committing early to a single answer, Honda is building a factory that can change its mind, and that was the smartest thing it announced all week.

What $3 billion buys

The plant will sit on land between Honda's Marysville and East Liberty factories, its fourth car plant in Ohio, with room for 240,000 vehicles a year and more than 2,000 jobs. Production is due in 2029, "pending final government approvals." Honda promises "the flexibility to produce multiple vehicle types and powertrains, including hybrid and gasoline-powered vehicles," plus battery-electric ones, and Noriya Kaihara, a Honda director and executive vice president, told analysts: "The new plant in Ohio will be designed to produce EVs in the future." It lifts Honda's US capacity to about 1.3 million vehicles a year, and its North American capacity from 1.67 million to 1.91 million. Bob Nelson, executive vice president of American Honda, called the package "one of the most significant commitments in our history." The rest of the day's news was mostly hybrid hardware, with no dollar figures attached.

What the detour cost

Honda itemizes the bill for the old plan. In the fiscal year that ended in March 2026, it booked ¥1,577.8 billion (about $10.0 billion) of EV-related losses, counting its share of affiliates' losses, most of it in the January–March quarter as it wrote down assets and changed strategy. This fiscal year it expects ¥520 billion more (about $3.3 billion), and the ¥484.8 billion from July to September is already 93% of that. By our arithmetic, that's about ¥2.1 trillion (about $13.3 billion) across 2 fiscal years, counting this year's forecast. Honda blames a changed EV market, which led it to cancel certain EV models it hasn't named; Bloomberg reports there were 3, all meant to be built in America.

Why the forecast still went up

Here's the strange part. Strip out the EV losses and July–September operating profit more than doubled, to ¥602.8 billion (about $3.8 billion). Leave them in and it fell 39.2%, to ¥117.9 billion (about $746 million). Honda then raised its full-year operating-profit forecast 20%, to ¥780 billion (about $4.9 billion), on strong motorcycle sales and more cars sold in North America. Some of the first half's strength won't repeat, including ¥97.0 billion (about $614 million) from tariff items such as refunds, and Honda warns that "the one-time profit increase recorded in the first half will not be a contributing factor in the second half."

The demand is already hybrid-shaped

The hybrid push isn't a guess. Hybrids are about 30% of Honda-brand sales in America and more than half of CR-V sales, Honda says. In October, Honda-brand hybrid sales hit 32,519, up 5.1% and a record for the month, and hybrids made up 36% of Civic sales, 45% of Accord sales and 53% of CR-V sales. Honda points to North American demand for "compact cars and fuel-efficient vehicles, including hybrid models, amid rising gasoline prices." Worldwide, its hybrid retail sales rose to 494,000 in the first half, from 454,000 a year earlier, and its briefing for analysts cited a goal of 2.5 million hybrids a year.

So the CR-V news is less a leap than a formality. The all-new 2028 CR-V arrives next year with only a new fifth-generation 2-motor hybrid, built in Ohio, Indiana and Ontario. It's on pace for more than 400,000 US sales for a third straight year, and if that pace holds, all of them will be hybrids. "With a 100% hybrid powertrain, the next-generation Honda CR-V will further expand the appeal of America's most popular SUV," said Luis Guerra, assistant vice president of Honda National Auto Sales. Honda has also promised 15 new Honda and Acura hybrids worldwide by 2030, and "multiple large hybrid models in the D segment and above," which its slides date from 2029. Bloomberg reports Honda is preparing hybrids for larger V6 models such as the Pilot and Acura MDX, though neither Bloomberg nor Honda says they'll be built at the new plant.

The expensive part wasn't betting on electric cars. It was signing up the suppliers before the buyers showed up.

In fairness to the electric car

Honda hasn't quit EVs, and it says so. "We are continuing fundamental EV development," Kaihara told analysts, and the new plant will be designed to build them. Bloomberg reports that Honda's battery joint venture with LG Energy Solution in Ohio began making cells this summer, even as Honda's planned battery complex in Canada has been on indefinite hold since May. The hybrid case has its own risk, too. Honda's explanation for hybrid demand leans on rising gasoline prices, which can fall, and a plant that opens in 2029 is building for a market nobody can see from here. A factory that could only build hybrids would be repeating the old mistake in a new color.

A factory that can change its mind

That's why the flexibility matters more than the hybrid. Honda's losses came from committing to specific electric models, and to the suppliers making parts for them, before the demand arrived; when it didn't, Honda had to pay to unwind them. The Ohio plant is the opposite kind of commitment: buildings, equipment and more than 2,000 jobs, with the product decision left open for years. Kaihara said that in North America, Honda intends "to establish a structure under which HEVs can be produced at each production site except Mexico." If the new plant builds hybrids when it opens and electric cars later, that won't be another U-turn. It'll be the building doing exactly what it was designed for.

The short version

In 1 week, Honda announced a $3 billion Ohio plant for 240,000 vehicles a year from 2029, booked ¥484.8 billion (about $3.1 billion) of EV-related losses in a quarter, raised its profit forecast and made the next CR-V hybrid-only. Its EV detour is on course to cost about ¥2.1 trillion (about $13.3 billion) over 2 fiscal years, counting this year's forecast. Hybrids are where the demand is today, but the new plant can build hybrids, gasoline cars or EVs, and that flexibility is the real lesson.
