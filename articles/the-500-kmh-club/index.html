The 500 km/h Club

In 1998, a McLaren F1 averaged 386.4 km/h on a test track in Germany and seemed untouchable. 28 years later, a 4-motor Chinese EV has gone 503 km/h on a highway in Abu Dhabi. The fastest cars, the arguments over how to measure them, and why the newest record comes with an asterisk.

By Camille Bouchard · 7 minute read

At 503.17 km/h (312.7 mph), a car covers about 140 meters every second, the length of a football field in about two-thirds of a second. On November 1, on a closed 10 km stretch of the E11 highway in the Al Dhafra region of Abu Dhabi, the German racing driver Marc Basseng drove a Yangwang U9 Xtreme that fast. No production car had been past 500 km/h before, and the one that did it is electric, built by China's BYD, and limited to 30 copies.

Then came the argument, because top speed has always come with one. The run was in a single direction. The traditional record is the average of 2 runs in opposite directions, and by that measure, the fastest production car is still an American one, from 2021.

How you measure the fastest car

A car running in 1 direction can be helped by a tailwind, a slight downhill or a smoother stretch of road. Running the same stretch in both directions and averaging the 2 speeds cancels most of that out, which is why the 2-way average became the standard for production-car records. Guinness World Records now lists the U9 Xtreme's run under its own title: "highest speed achieved by a production car in a single-direction run." It was measured with the car's speedometer and a calibrated VBOX data logger and reviewed by an FIA representative. And Guinness is careful to say it "should not be confused with conventional fastest-production-car records, which require different testing protocols, including runs in opposite directions."

The modern chase starts at Volkswagen's Ehra-Lessien test track in Germany on March 31, 1998, when Andy Wallace averaged 240.1 mph (386.4 km/h) in both directions in a McLaren F1 with its rev limiter raised. For almost 7 years, no production car beat it. Then, in February 2005, Koenigsegg's CCR reached 387.87 km/h at the Nardò test track in Italy, and later that year Volkswagen's Bugatti arrived with the Veyron, which the testing agency TÜV measured at 407 km/h in both directions. SSC, a small company from Washington state, took the record in 2007 with its Ultimate Aero, at 412.28 km/h on a closed highway, and Bugatti took it back in 2010 with the Veyron Super Sport, at 431.07 km/h. Guinness briefly suspended that record in 2013, because customer cars were limited to 415 km/h, then reinstated it.

In 2017, on a closed stretch of Highway 160 near Pahrump, Nevada, Koenigsegg's Agera RS averaged 447.19 km/h (277.87 mph), touching 457.94 km/h in 1 direction. In 2019, Andy Wallace was back at Ehra-Lessien in a pre-production Chiron Super Sport 300+ and went 490.48 km/h (304.77 mph) in 1 direction. "Never before has a series manufacturer reached this high speed," Bugatti said. Then it stopped chasing top speed.

SSC claimed an average of 316.11 mph (508.73 km/h) for its Tuatara in October 2020. It later admitted, "we did not reach the originally claimed speeds of 331 MPH or even 301 MPH in October of 2020." In January 2021, on the runway at the Kennedy Space Center in Florida, the Tuatara averaged 282.9 mph (455.3 km/h) in both directions. That's still the 2-way record. In 2022 it hit 295 mph in 1 direction, but the return run was aborted, so no record.

Then the electric cars arrived

For most of that history, electric cars weren't part of the conversation. That changed quickly. In 2022, Rimac's Nevera reached 412 km/h at the Papenburg test track in Germany. In 2024, Marc Basseng drove an Aspark Owl prototype to 438.7 km/h there. In August 2025, he took a Yangwang U9 Track Edition to 472.41 km/h, and the next month he drove a U9 Xtreme to 496.22 km/h in 1 direction.

The U9 Xtreme has 4 electric motors that spin to 30,000 rpm and deliver up to 555 kW each, 2,220 kW in all, or about 2,977 hp. It runs a 1,200-volt system, where the standard U9 uses 800 volts, and its LFP Blade battery can deliver up to 1,800 kW. BYD hasn't published its weight; working from its stated power-to-weight figure, it's about 2,480 kg (about 5,470 lb). For the Abu Dhabi run, it wore semi-slick tires developed with Giti, and between runs, engineers adjusted battery and motor temperatures, rear-wing angle, suspension software and tire pressures.

The asterisk

Critics weren't impressed by the method. "A record has to be run both ways, and this one was run in a single direction," wrote Autonext, which said BYD ran 4 times over 2 days without ever setting a 2-way average. Top Gear put an asterisk in its headline. Forbes pointed out that it isn't clear whether customer cars will get the same tires, or reach the same speed. None of that changes the number. An electric car weighing about 2,480 kg (5,470 lb) has gone faster than any production car before it, in 1 direction, on a public road. Whether that's a record depends on which rules you think count.

Who chases speed now

The list of record-holders says something about the car industry at each moment. In 1998, it was a British supercar with a BMW V12. For the next 20 years, it was mostly Bugatti, then owned by Volkswagen, trading blows with small, stubborn specialists from Sweden and America. Now it's a Chinese carmaker best known for affordable EVs, using a top-speed run to show the world what its batteries and motors can do. The 500 km/h club has 1 member, with an asterisk. The interesting question is who tries to join it next, and whether they'll run it both ways.

The short version

The Yangwang U9 Xtreme reached 503.17 km/h (312.7 mph) in Abu Dhabi on November 1, the first production car past 500 km/h. It was a 1-way run, which Guinness lists separately, and the traditional 2-way record is still the SSC Tuatara's 455.3 km/h from 2021. In 28 years, the record has gone from a McLaren F1 at 386.4 km/h to a 4-motor electric car, and from Germany's test tracks to closed public highways.
