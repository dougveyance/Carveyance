The original Fiesta — Project Bobcat — was Ford's first modern front-wheel-drive car and the biggest bet the company had made since the war. America got the German-built version from 1978: a 1.6 Kent crossflow smothered in catalysts and air pumps, round sealed-beam lamps, big federal bumpers, and Base, Decor, Sport and Ghia trims. None of the equipment hurt it much, because there was nothing to haul: at 832 kg (1,835 lb), the Fiesta S did 0–60 in under 11 seconds and an honest 98 mph while sipping fuel — quicker than some V8 sedans of the day.

## Killed by arithmetic

The Fiesta existed because of fuel-economy pressure, and fuel-economy rules killed it anyway: as an import, only about 75,000 a year could count toward Ford's CAFE averages, and from 1980 none at all. Ford needed a countable domestic small car, so the US-built Escort replaced the Fiesta and the Pinto for 1981, and the badge left America for 30 years. European Mk1s became hot-hatch ancestors through the XR2; the US cars mostly just rusted away.

## Buying one now

Survivors are rare and wonderful. Rust decides everything — arches, sills, floors — because mechanical parts are simple and the Kent engine is one of the most supported old 4-cylinders on earth thanks to Formula Ford. Prices remain curiosity-level: solid drivers in the low-to-mid 4 figures, the best cars barely more, and ratty ones nearly free. Buy the most complete, least rusty car in reach, keep it stock-ish, and enjoy owning the reason the 2011 return meant something.
