Why Mercedes Says a German Work Hour Costs Up to 5 Times More

Mercedes-Benz froze investment in its German plants on November 12, saying an hour of production work there costs up to 5 times as much as at sites in Eastern Europe. Europe's official figures put the gap at about 3.7 times Hungary and 4.8 times Romania. What Germany really costs, what doesn't explain it, and where Mercedes already builds instead.

By Grayson Kollen · 8 minute read

In 2011, Germany built 5.87 million passenger cars, its peak. Last year it built 4.15 million, 29.3% fewer. On November 12, Mercedes-Benz told its German workers it would stop putting new money into their factories. In an interview posted for employees, production chief Michael Schiebe said the board had "jointly decided to halt all further investments in our German locations," after talks with the works council and the IG Metall union on cutting labor costs stalled.

No plant has been named. "We all want to avoid plant closures at all costs," said Britta Seeger, the board member for human resources. "Unfortunately, however, we must now explore this option in more detail." Schiebe first warned in September that without savings, Mercedes would have to close 1 German assembly plant and 1 powertrain plant. The company's case rests on a single number: an hour of production work in Germany costs, in some cases, up to 5 times as much as at sites in Eastern Europe. We checked it, along with the other usual suspects, hours and energy.

Why now

Mercedes isn't arguing from comfort. Its car division earned an adjusted €560 million (about $627 million) in the third quarter, half what it made a year earlier. Its China sales fell 31% in the quarter, and on October 27 it cut this year's forecast for the car business to a 2% to 3% return on sales. Germany's car industry, meanwhile, employed an average of 731,928 people last year, 41,021 fewer than the year before, according to the VDA, its trade association.

Checking the 5 times

By coincidence, Eurostat, the European Union's statistics office, released its newest survey of car-industry labor costs on the same day Mercedes froze its spending. It measures what an hour of work costs a vehicle maker, pay plus everything on top. In 2024, that hour cost €66.22 in Germany (about $74, or $80 in today's money), the most of any country here.

On that measure, a German hour costs about 3.7 times a Hungarian one, 3.8 times a Polish one and 4.8 times a Romanian one. So "up to 5 times" fits Romania's national figure, not Hungary's. Chief executive Ola Källenius tells a similar story with an index he laid out in Berlin this month: Germany 100, the US 80, Hungary 30, Romania 20 and China between 10 and 20. On his scale, Germany costs 1.25 times the US, 3.3 times Hungary and 5 times Romania.

The gap is closing, but only as a ratio. From 2020 to 2024, the hourly cost rose 12.9% in Germany, 45.4% in Hungary and 57.4% in Romania, so a German hour went from about 4.7 times a Hungarian one to about 3.7 times. In euros, the gap per hour actually widened a little, because 12.9% of a big number is still more than 45% of a small one.

Hours, not just wages

Pay is half the argument. The other half is time. IG Metall's contracts set a 35-hour week. Mercedes' supervisory board chairman, Martin Brudermüller, called in June for a return to 40, and the package on the table when talks broke down included longer hours without extra pay, plus changes to bonuses and voluntary benefits.

The arithmetic of a longer week is simple, and smaller than it sounds. Working 40 hours instead of 35 for the same pay is 14.3% more time, which cuts the cost of each hour by 12.5%. On Källenius' scale, that takes Germany from 100 to 87.5. He wants 80: "from 100 to 80 is achievable," he said. A longer week delivers only 62.5% of that cut, and the package suggests where Mercedes would look for the rest: what workers take home. That has already started: about 90,000 of Mercedes' roughly 108,000 employees in Germany saw a special payment expected in July pushed to next year.

Not the power bill

Then there's energy. For a large industrial user, Eurostat puts Germany's electricity price in the first half of 2026 at 14.6 euro cents per kilowatt-hour before VAT (about 16.4 US cents). That's 17.0% less than in Hungary, 13.2% less than in Poland and 8.4% less than in Romania, though 8.8% above the EU average and well above France and Spain. Against Western Europe, German power is expensive. Against Hungary, Poland and Romania, it's cheaper. Energy isn't what separates Germany from Hungary. Labor is.

Where Mercedes already builds

Mercedes doesn't have to imagine life outside Germany. It already lives there.

The A-Class left Rastatt for Kecskemét this year, and the expanded Hungarian plant, a project of about €1 billion (about $1.12 billion) that took the site from 200 to 440 hectares (about 1,087 acres), opened on July 13 with the electric C-Class. The GLC for North America moves from Bremen to Tuscaloosa from late 2027, partly because of tariffs, its North American chief, Jason Hoff, told Reuters; Mercedes is putting $4 billion into Alabama through 2030. Mercedes has said "the low-cost country share will be increased from 15 to 30 percent by 2027."

But the traffic runs both ways: the electric CLA moved from Kecskemét to Rastatt. And a cheap plant is only cheap when it's busy: Hungary's Portfolio.hu reported in February, citing a 24.hu report based on industry and internal sources, that Kecskemét had run below half its capacity for months and put some areas on a single shift early this year.

The other side of the table

IG Metall's answer is that the hourly rate is the wrong place to start. "Anyone who wants to talk seriously about competitiveness must not start with working conditions," the union's president, Christiane Benner, said in July. "The 35-hour workweek is not obsolete, good collective bargaining agreements are not a competitive disadvantage," said Barbara Resch, who leads IG Metall in Baden-Württemberg, Mercedes' home state, in the same statement. Ergun Lümali, Mercedes' general works council chair, said then: "The company's actions are causing massive uncertainty and demotivating many colleagues." Workers also have an agreement, from March 2025, that extends job security at Mercedes in Germany to the end of 2034, with no compulsory layoffs.

Källenius says turning Germany into Romania or Hungary isn't the plan; he called the idea "Schwachsinn," nonsense. "We don't want a rotten compromise," he added. "We want real change." He's also open to an IG Metall idea from the current bargaining round: temporary company-level departures from collective standards in exchange for binding commitments on investment, sites, jobs and training.

What the math says

Strip away the speeches and the numbers agree. Germany's problem isn't the power bill, which is lower than in the East, and it isn't only the 35-hour week, which is worth 12.5 points on Källenius' scale. It's the hour itself, about 3.7 times Hungary's and 4.8 times Romania's on Europe's own figures. Mercedes isn't trying to close that gap. It wants 20% off the German hour, down to American levels, and is betting that keeps German plants worth investing in. IG Metall is betting they're worth their price already. Until either side gives ground, Mercedes' new money stays out of Germany.

The short version

Mercedes froze investment in its German plants and says a production hour there costs up to 5 times as much as in Eastern Europe. Eurostat's car-industry figures say about 3.7 times Hungary and 4.8 times Romania. Energy isn't the difference: German industrial power is cheaper than in the East. A 40-hour week would cut the hourly cost by 12.5%, short of the 20% Mercedes wants, and IG Metall says working conditions are the wrong place to start.
