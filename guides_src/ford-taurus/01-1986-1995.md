The 1986 Taurus was the product gamble that saved Ford: an aerodynamic front-driver with no grille, developed with Deming's statistical quality methods, launched into a showroom world of vinyl-roofed boxes. The press swooned — Car of the Year, a Ten Best spot — and buyers followed, 200,000-plus in year 1 and 2 million built by the end of 1991. The 1992 second generation smoothed the same bones, added standard dual airbags by 1994, and took the best-seller crown from the Honda Accord with 409,751 sales — holding it 5 straight years.

## What they're like now

These are 30-to-40-year-old American sedans, which means survivors divide into cherished originals and $800 beaters with nothing in between. The 140-hp Vulcan is agricultural but nearly unkillable; the 3.8 Essex brought torque and a head-gasket reputation worth checking; the early AXOD automatics want evidence of fluid care. Wagons have a cult. Rust decides everything, as always — floors, arches, subframes — and a clean southern GL is a genuine slice of 1990s Americana for low-4-figure money.

## Which to seek

A first-generation car in good original condition is the historically interesting one — the actual revolution, increasingly welcome at shows. The second generation is the better daily driver: more refinement, more safety, same honest engineering. Either way, buy on condition and completeness rather than year or trim, because parts support favors the common stuff and originality is what tomorrow's collectors will pay for. The fast ones are a separate religion, covered next.
