In 1989 Ford shipped a family sedan whose intake manifold belonged in a museum. Yamaha had transformed the 140-hp Vulcan into a 220-hp, 7,000-rpm DOHC V6, Ford added a 5-speed and subtle bodywork, and Car and Driver ran the SHO to 60 in 6.6 seconds with an observed 143 mph — then compared it to the 1964 Mustang. Planned as a 1-year special, it sold over 15,000 copies immediately and became an institution: the 3.2 automatic joined for 1993, and the 1996 car became the only V8 Taurus ever, a 235-hp 3.4 with Yamaha heads and a Cosworth-cast block.

## Buying the classics

**1989–91:** the one history remembers — manual only, lightest, purest. Hunt for stock cars with service records; the Yamaha engine is robust but cam-sprocket welds are the famous check on neglected examples, and interior plastics are unobtainium. Values have left beater territory for good. **1992–95:** same thrills, friendlier prices, automatic available. **1996–99 V8:** a genuine rarity — about 9,000 sold in its best year — whose camshaft-sprocket failure reputation makes documented preventive welding the single question that decides any purchase.

## The comeback

After a decade off, the 2010 SHO returned pronounced "show": a 365-hp twin-turbo EcoBoost with standard AWD at $37,995 — about $58,600 in 2026 dollars. The 2013 refresh car ran 5.2 to 60 in Car and Driver's hands, the quickest Taurus ever. Used, they're the sleeper's sleeper: big, subtle, brutally quick in a straight line, and priced like ordinary used sedans because the badge never told anyone otherwise. Check the water pump (internal, engine-out to replace — service records matter) and the PTU fluid on any AWD car, then enjoy the last word in Q-ship Fords.
