Honda Isn't Treating the Hybrid as a Bridge Anymore

Honda hasn't given up on the electric car. But its plan for the rest of the decade runs on hybrids that are cheaper, lighter, more efficient and, Honda promises, more enjoyable to drive.

By Elliot Vance · 7 minute read

Honda has given its next-generation hybrid system about the plainest name possible: Honda Hybrid. It sounds as if the marketing department gave up before lunch. The engineering and the business plan behind it are a lot more interesting.

Starting in 2027, Honda plans 15 models with the system by the end of its fiscal year in March 2030, most of them sold primarily in North America. They'll sit on a lighter, stiffer platform, with more efficient engines, new control software and an electric all-wheel-drive system. Honda announced it on October 9 as part of the plan, laid out in May, to rebuild its car business by moving development and production toward hybrids. A 15-model rollout and a new hybrid for its biggest vehicles is a lot more than a placeholder. Honda is building its car business around hybrids for the rest of the decade.

How Honda's hybrid works

Most hybrids combine the engine and electric motors through a transmission or a power-split device. Honda's 2-motor system, which it calls e:HEV, works differently. In much of everyday driving at low and medium speeds, the gasoline engine runs a generator while an electric motor turns the wheels, so the car behaves like an EV with its own power plant on board. At higher speeds, where driving the wheels directly is more efficient, a clutch connects the engine to the wheels. That's why the Accord Hybrid and CR-V Hybrid feel so smooth and respond so quickly: electric power does most of the work, but the battery can stay small because the engine keeps topping it up. The next system refines that formula rather than replacing it.

Lighter, cheaper, more efficient

In a technology briefing in December 2024, Honda said its next mid-size platform would be about 90 kg (198 lb) lighter than today's, with new 1.5- and 2.0-liter direct-injection, Atkinson-cycle engines and a smaller drive unit. It targeted a fuel-economy gain of more than 10% and a system cost up to 50% lower than a comparable 2018 Honda hybrid. Losing weight helps almost everything at once: efficiency, braking, handling, tire wear and performance. Cost may matter even more to Honda's recovery. A cheaper hybrid system lets Honda put it across more of the lineup instead of saving it for expensive trims, and that's the difference between selling a few efficient alternatives and making the hybrid the default.

Honda hasn't said which model comes first. Nikkei Asia has reported that a new CR-V Hybrid could enter production in Canada in February 2027, with a new 2.0-liter engine and a production cost about 30% lower. Honda hasn't confirmed it.

Electric all-wheel drive is more than traction

The new electric all-wheel drive deserves attention. Today's all-wheel-drive Honda hybrids, such as the CR-V Hybrid, send power to the rear wheels mechanically, through a driveshaft. A separate electric motor at the rear removes the driveshaft and can send power to the back wheels faster and more precisely. Toyota has used versions of the idea for years. Honda says its unit can be shared with its EVs, should cut cost and complexity, and should improve performance in bad weather. Combined with Honda's new Motion Management System, a rear motor could also help turn the car into a corner and recover energy when it slows down, which would make all-wheel drive part of how the hybrid drives, not just an extra pair of driven wheels. That could matter a great deal in future SUVs, and perhaps in performance-minded Acuras.

The question isn't whether the hybrid is the final form of the car. It's whether it's the right one for the next generation of buyers.

The big Hondas are the real opportunity

Honda's hybrid strength today is in the Civic, Accord and CR-V. Its larger American vehicles, the Pilot, Passport and Ridgeline, have no hybrid at all. That's expected to change. Honda has shown a larger system with a newly developed V6, new electric motors and an upgraded battery for its full-size SUVs, and has said those SUVs will be 30% more fuel-efficient than today's non-hybrid full-size SUVs in the US, InsideEVs reported. Honda hasn't named the vehicles that will get it. The Pilot, Passport, Ridgeline and larger Acuras are logical candidates, but that's an inference, not a schedule. If Honda can give a midsize American SUV the towing and long-distance highway performance buyers expect while cutting its fuel use, it will be selling into a very receptive market: buyers want better mileage, and many still don't want the charging, the weight or the price of a big EV.

After a painful EV retreat

Honda's hybrid push can't be separated from its rethink on electric cars. On March 12, Honda canceled 3 EVs it had planned to build in the US, the Honda 0 SUV, the 0 Saloon and the Acura RSX, and said losses tied to the change could reach 2.5 trillion yen (about $15.7 billion). Its American EV sales had leaned on the Prologue, which GM builds for it, and in July Honda said the Prologue would end after 2026, leaving it with no EV in the US. That doesn't mean Honda has given up on battery-electric cars. It means Honda no longer expects EV sales to grow fast enough to support its old product and investment plan. Hybrids are the quicker answer: they use the fuel stations that already exist, fit how people already drive and cut fuel use without asking anyone to plan their life around a charger.

More than a bridge, whatever Honda calls it

Honda still describes the hybrid as part of a transition, and technically that may be right. But car-industry transitions can last decades. A model launched in 2027 may stay in production into the 2030s and on the road into the 2040s. The real question isn't whether the hybrid is the final form of the car. It's whether it's the most practical technology for the next generation of buyers, and Honda is betting it is. The name Honda Hybrid may be painfully ordinary. The strategy behind it could decide whether Honda gets back its reputation as one of the smartest engineering companies in the business, or spends another decade reacting to everyone else.

The short version

Honda's next-generation system, Honda Hybrid, arrives in 2027, with 15 models by March 2030, mostly for North America. Honda says the new platform is about 90 kg (198 lb) lighter, targets more than 10% better fuel economy at up to half the cost of its 2018 system, and adds electric all-wheel drive. It follows Honda's cancellation of 3 US-built EVs in March, and its biggest opportunity is the large SUVs and pickups that have never had a Honda hybrid.
