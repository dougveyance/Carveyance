Few cars came back carrying more expectations than the Supra. By the time Toyota revived the name for 2020, enthusiasts had turned the fourth-generation car into a monument to tuning culture. The new one arrived with a turbocharged straight-six, rear-wheel drive, 2 seats and BMW fingerprints on nearly every important mechanical part. For some people, that was enough to dismiss it before they ever turned the key. For everyone else, it was a chance to judge the actual car instead of the internet's idea of what it should have been.

The fifth-generation Supra is small, quick, responsive and far more serious than its curvy styling suggests. It's also a peculiar daily driver. Visibility is limited, cargo space is modest, the cabin fits snugly, and wind buffeting with the windows down became one of its better-known irritations. Some cars invite you in. The Supra asks whether you remembered to stretch first.

## What changed under the long hood

The first 2020 cars made 335 hp. For 2021, Toyota raised the 3.0-liter engine to 382 hp and 368 lb-ft, a big change that's easy to miss in used listings because the cars look the same. The same year brought a lighter, cheaper 2.0-liter turbo 4-cylinder with 255 hp. It was sold from 2021 through 2024, with an automatic only, and it gets its own chapter in this guide.

Every early Supra was an automatic. The 6-speed manual arrived for the 2023 model year, on the 3.0 only, and it changes the relationship between car and driver more than any power increase could. It isn't the quickest version. Toyota quotes 4.2 seconds to 60 mph for the manual and 3.9 for the automatic. But it makes ordinary drives more involving.

Toyota kept refining the steering, suspension and differential along the way. The last of them, the 2026 MkV Final Edition, was limited to 1,300 cars in the US, and it was about control, not another horsepower number: the same 382 hp, with larger Brembo brakes, revised suspension and a stiffer body.

## On a road worth remembering

A short wheelbase, wide tires and plenty of torque make the Supra feel alert. Its strongest talent is gathering speed without much apparent effort. The 3.0 can turn a modest gap in traffic into a much shorter one with a single squeeze of the throttle. The nose follows instructions eagerly, but this is a car that rewards measured inputs, not a theatrical right foot. In the wet, the condition of the tires matters. So does the driver's judgment.

The tradeoffs are the usual sports-car ones, only packed more tightly. A tall driver should try the seat and the roof clearance before falling for the outside. Small windows and thick pillars make city driving less relaxed than in a GR86. Road noise, firm damping and limited practicality come with the deal. Toyota hasn't found a loophole that turns a compact rear-drive coupe into an SUV on the way home from work.

## The buying decision

For the strongest value, start with a documented 2021–2022 3.0 automatic. It has the 382-hp engine without the premium a manual or a last-year car tends to carry. A 2023-or-later manual is the better pick for anyone who sees shifting as part of the point. The 2.0 can be a lot of fun, but its price needs to be clearly lower than a 3.0's to make sense.

The cheapest Supra isn't necessarily the cheapest Supra to own. Its performance tires, brakes and specialized parts aren't Corolla consumables, and signs of hard launches or skipped fluid changes should count for more than a spotless set of wheels. Look at the repair history, any track use or modifications, and the original equipment, not the legend of a different Supra generation.

## A verdict without nostalgia goggles

The modern Supra is a fine sports car precisely because it doesn't try to reproduce every detail of the old one. The BMW partnership is both the source of its excellent powertrain and the subject of an identity debate that will never end. If you want an unfiltered, low-cost back-road companion, look hard at the GR86. If you want something roomier for long trips, the Nissan Z is worth a drive. The Supra is for the driver who wants serious straight-six performance in a compact coupe and is prepared to pay for it.

There's real Toyota character here, especially in the later chassis changes. It just shows up in engineering decisions, not in a parts catalog stamped entirely in Japan.

::: curiosity A Japanese Legend, Born in Austria
Every fifth-generation Supra was built by Magna Steyr in Graz, Austria, on the same line as its BMW Z4 sibling. Because BMW builds the car, it's BMW, not Toyota, that files the Supra's safety recalls with US regulators. One of Toyota's most Japanese nameplates came back with an Austrian birthplace and a German engine. The family-reunion paperwork must have been something.
:::
