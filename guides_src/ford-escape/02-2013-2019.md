For 2013 the boxy Escape became a swoopy global car — the same vehicle Europe called the Kuga, built in Louisville under One Ford. The hybrid and V6 left; EcoBoost turbos arrived. America's verdict was 295,993 sales in year 1 and then 4 straight years over 300,000, peaking at 308,296 in 2017. More than 2 million third-generation Escapes went to US driveways, which is why they are the cheapest path into a modern compact crossover today.

## The engine ladder, best to avoid

The **2.0 EcoBoost**, 237 hp, is the keeper: about 7 seconds to 60, 1,588 kg (3,500 lb) of towing with the tow package, and 24 mpg combined with all-wheel drive. The **2.5** base engine is slow but simple — the fleet special, and a fine cheap commuter. The **1.5 EcoBoost** (2017–19) is the better small turbo, part of the refresh that also brought Sync 3 and the Edge-style face. The **1.6 EcoBoost** (2013–16) is the one to research before buying: it drew multiple recalls for overheating and engine-fire risk, and while repaired cars have run fine for a decade, an incomplete recall on the VIN report is a walk-away.

## Years and money

The 2017–2019 cars are the sweet spot — refreshed cabin, 1.5 or 2.0 power, and prices that have finished the steep part of depreciation. A 2013 S started at $23,295, about $33,600 in 2026 dollars; today the same car is a fraction of that, and even clean late 2.0 Titaniums sit in the mid-teens. Check 2014 models for the door-latch recall, confirm all recall work by VIN, and on any EcoBoost insist on evidence of regular oil changes — small turbos forgive little neglect. The sales chart in the checklist chapter shows why patience pays: with this many sold, there is always another one for sale tomorrow.
