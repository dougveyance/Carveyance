The most important Escape ever built had no V6 and no badge drama. The 2005 Escape Hybrid was Ford's first hybrid-electric vehicle of any kind, and the first hybrid SUV offered in the US by any manufacturer — a 2.3 Atkinson-cycle engine, an electric motor, a CVT, 155 hp combined and 27 mpg combined in a body whose V6 version returned 18. Ford's 2004 patent-sharing accord with Toyota kept the lawyers calm, and the engineering spoke for itself.

## The taxi proof

The durability question hanging over every early hybrid — what happens when the battery ages? — was answered by taxi fleets. New York and San Francisco put Escape Hybrids into the hardest service passenger cars see, and they routinely cleared 300,000 miles on original packs. Ford sold 122,850 hybrids through 2012, the second generation lifting combined output to 177 hp and 32 mpg. Then the 2013 redesign dropped the hybrid entirely — an 8-year gap that in hindsight looks like Ford handing the segment to the RAV4 Hybrid.

## The return, and the plug

The 2020 generation brought it back properly: 200 hp combined (restated as 192 from 2023 under new SAE measurement), 40 mpg combined with all-wheel drive available, and MotorWeek's test clocking 8.0 seconds to 60 — brisker than any base-engine Escape before it. The first Escape Plug-In Hybrid joined it: a 14.4-kWh battery, about 37 miles of electric range, 101 MPGe combined, front-drive only. For a 2-car household that can charge at home, the PHEV does school runs on electrons and road trips at 40 mpg.

Used, the hybrids are the pick of every year they exist. The 2005–2012 cars are elderly now — buy on pack health and rust, not miles alone — while 2020-onward hybrids have shown little of note beyond routine recalls. New, the 2026 Hybrid at 39 mpg combined is the single best way to spend Escape money in the nameplate's final year.
