Decades in a Second

A badge is the most efficient piece of storytelling ever invented. Two centimeters of enamel can carry a war, a merger, a family crest, a dead pilot or a gear tooth — and your brain unpacks all of it before you have consciously registered that you looked.

By Margot Devries · Carveyance · 19 min read

A man walks past you on the street. In roughly the time it takes to blink, you have read him.

Small embroidered polo player on the shirt: Ralph Lauren. You conclude — without deciding to — something about money, or the desire to signal it. Keys come out of his pocket and there is an iron symbol on the fob: Volvo. You revise. Now he is sensible. Safety-conscious. Probably has children, or intends to. Likes things that are well made and quiet about it. You have adjusted your entire model of a stranger based on two small pieces of decoration, and you did it in under two seconds without once forming a sentence about it.

Neither of those marks told you anything. A polo player is a man on a horse. The Volvo badge is a circle with an arrow. Everything you "read" was assembled from decades of accumulated association that you absorbed passively, for free, without ever studying it.

That is what an emblem is for. It is a compression algorithm for reputation.

The badge takes a second to read. It took eighty years to write.The whole trick, in one line

And it works on everything. A star on a football helmet and you are somewhere in the mythology of Texas. A green mermaid and you know the price of the coffee before you see the menu. A bitten apple and you have a set of opinions about design, price and the person carrying it. The Statue of Liberty does not mean "New York," it means an entire tangle of arrival, ambition, freedom and noise.

Cars just happen to be the field where this is done best, because a car badge has to survive being seen at sixty miles an hour, in the rain, from behind, by someone who is not paying attention.

Part oneWhat the research actually says

Marketing loves to tell you that color has fixed, universal, almost magical meanings. Most of that is nonsense dressed as science. But underneath the nonsense there is a real and rather interesting body of work, and it is worth separating the two.

The number you see quoted everywhere comes from Satyendra Singh's 2006 paper in Management Decision, titled "Impact of color on marketing." Singh found that people make up their minds about a product within about ninety seconds of first seeing it, and that somewhere between 62 and 90 percent of that snap assessment is based on color alone.

The more careful work is Lauren Labrecque and George Milne's 2012 paper in the Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science, which carries the best title in the field: "Exciting red and competent blue." Across four studies they mapped colors onto brand personality dimensions and found consistent effects — red reliably reads as exciting, blue reliably reads as competent — and, more usefully, that saturation and brightness amplify those traits independently of hue.

Here is the part the listicles leave out.

The honest version

Bottomley and Doyle's work found that what matters is not the color itself but the perceived appropriateness of that color for that product. Blue is not inherently trustworthy. Blue is trustworthy for a bank. The same blue on a hot sauce bottle reads as wrong, and "wrong" is worse than any color choice.

And Madden, Hewett and Roth's cross-national study found that color meanings shift by culture. White is purity in one market and mourning in another. There is no universal color code. There is only fit — and fit is local.

So when someone tells you a color "means" something, the accurate translation is: in this market, for this category, that color currently fits. Which is a much less exciting sentence and a much more useful one.

Part twoWhy hospitals are green

The best evidence that color choices are functional rather than mystical is the one people cite most often as proof of the opposite.

Surgeons used to wear white. Of course they did — white is cleanliness, purity, professionalism, and it shows you have nothing to hide. But under the ferocious lighting of an operating theater, white creates two problems.

The first is glare. The second is stranger, and it is the real reason.

Stare at something red for a long time and the red-sensitive photoreceptors in your retina fatigue. Look away at a white surface and you briefly see a ghost of the opposite color — a green-cyan smear floating over whatever you are now looking at. This is the afterimage effect, and if you are a surgeon whose entire visual field has been red tissue for three hours, those ghosts drift across your colleague's white coat at exactly the moment you need to see clearly.

In 1914, an influential American surgeon — usually named as Harry Sherman — switched his operating clothes to green. Green sits opposite red on the color wheel, which means the afterimage lands on green and simply vanishes into it. The ghost has nowhere to appear.

Green also happens to look calm, and hospitals have leaned into that ever since. But calm was the side effect. The reason was optics.

Hospital green was not chosen to soothe you. It was chosen so a surgeon could stop seeing ghosts.Complementary colors, 1914

And why fast food is red and yellow

Look at McDonald's, Burger King, KFC, Wendy's, In-N-Out, Five Guys. The palette is almost aggressively consistent: red and yellow, occasionally with white.

The usual explanation is that red stimulates appetite, which is stated far more confidently than the evidence supports. The more defensible account is simpler and has two parts. Red and yellow are among the most visible color combinations at distance and speed — which matters enormously when your customer is doing 45 mph and deciding in three seconds. And red is an arousal color: it raises attention and urgency, which suits a business model built on turnover rather than lingering.

Note what fine dining does instead. Dark wood, deep green, cream, black, brass. Low arousal. Slow down. Order another bottle.

Brown behaves differently again. It reads as earth, wood, coffee, chocolate, leather, bread — warmth, age and comfort — which is why it dominates food packaging that wants to feel handmade, and why it disappeared from car interiors during the era when everyone wanted to look futuristic and came roaring back the moment they wanted to look expensive.

Red — arousal, urgency, speed
Orange — warning, play, energy
Blue — competence, calm, trust
Green — recovery, nature, safety
Brown — comfort, age, craft

Broad associations in Western markets. They are conventions, not constants — and they move.

Part threeThe car color paradox

All of which sets up the funniest statistic in the industry.

Manufacturers spend fortunes on color. Ferrari built a religion on red. Lamborghini will sell you a car in a shade of green normally reserved for highlighter pens. Jeep leans hard on oranges and yellows because bright means play, and play means Wrangler.

Then you look at what the world actually buys.

Red, brown/beige, green and yellow/gold together account for the remainder. Regionally: North America runs white 31%, blue 10%, red 7%. Europe is grayer still, with gray leading at 26%.

Seventy-four percent of the cars built on this planet are white, black or gray. Three quarters of the world, given a free choice and an entire spectrum, picks no color at all.

Which tells you something honest about color psychology: it operates powerfully on how you feel about a brand, and considerably less on what you buy when the object costs forty thousand dollars, has to be resold in four years, and will sit outside your house where the neighbors can see it.

Color is emotional. Purchasing is defensive. The badge, meanwhile, works on both.

Part fourFour kinds of badge

Spend long enough looking and every automotive emblem falls into one of four families. The family tells you what the company was thinking about at the moment it needed a mark.

The fourth family is the smallest and, to my eye, the most interesting — because those badges are not making a claim at all. They are showing you evidence.

Part fiveTen badges and where they came from

Ferrari — a dead pilot and a grieving countess

The prancing horse was not Enzo's idea and did not begin on a car. It belonged to Francesco Baracca, Italy's leading fighter ace of the First World War, who painted a black horse on the side of his aircraft. Baracca was killed in 1918. Enzo Ferrari later met his parents, and Baracca's mother, Countess Paolina, suggested he put her son's horse on his cars for luck. Ferrari added the canary yellow behind it — the civic color of Modena, his home town, and the SF beneath the horse for Scuderia Ferrari.

So the most famous badge in motoring is a war memorial with a superstition attached, on a background chosen out of local pride. It is not a logo. It is a small act of remembrance that happens to sell cars.

BMW — the propeller that never was

Everybody knows the BMW roundel is a spinning propeller against blue sky, a nod to the company's aircraft-engine origins. Everybody is wrong.

BMW Group Classic's own historians have been patiently explaining for years that the roundel came from the circular emblem of Rapp Motorenwerke, BMW's predecessor, with the quartered blue and white of the Bavarian state colors dropped into the middle. The propeller story comes from a 1929 advertisement that showed the roundel inside a spinning propeller — an ad man's flourish, seven years after the logo already existed.

The myth is now so much more famous than the truth that BMW has largely stopped fighting it. Which is its own lesson: a badge's meaning is not decided by the company. It is decided by everyone else.

Mercedes-Benz — a three-way claim on the planet

Gottlieb Daimler once marked his house on a postcard with a three-pointed star. His sons later explained that the three points stood for his ambition to build engines for land, sea and air. After Daimler-Motoren-Gesellschaft merged with Benz & Cie. in 1926, the star was combined with the Benz laurel wreath.

Which makes the Mercedes badge less a logo than a territorial declaration. Not "we make cars." More: our engines will move everything, everywhere. Remarkably, they were not exaggerating.

Audi — four rings and a man who was thrown out of his own company

The four rings are the four firms that merged into Auto Union in 1932: Audi, DKW, Horch and Wanderer. Linked, because they linked. It is an honest, unromantic, extremely German badge.

The good part is the name. August Horch founded Horch, fell out with his own board and left. Barred from using his surname on a new company, he took the Latin. Horch is the German imperative "listen." Audi is the Latin imperative "listen." He built a rival firm and named it a translation of himself, and both companies ended up inside the same four rings anyway.

Subaru — a merger, written as a constellation

Most merger badges are arithmetic. Subaru’s is astronomy.

Subaru is the Japanese name for the Pleiades, and the word carries a second sense of gathering or uniting. Fuji Heavy Industries was itself assembled from several companies, and the badge says so out loud: one large star, five smaller ones.

It is the same statement Audi makes with four rings, told by somebody looking up instead of down — and it suits a company that has always had a small-group-of-people-who-know quality to it.

Alfa Romeo — a cross, a serpent, and a person

Since 1910 the Alfa badge has carried two symbols of Milan side by side: the red cross of the city, and the Biscione, the serpent from the coat of arms of the Visconti, who ruled the place. "MILANO" sat on the badge for over sixty years, until 1972.

And yes, the serpent is consuming a human figure. Interpretations vary — a child, a defeated Saracen, a symbol of rebirth, a heraldic convention that nobody has satisfactorily explained. Alfa Romeo has never fully resolved it and, to their enormous credit, has never removed it either.

Porsche — a medieval résumé

The crest, drawn up in 1952, is two coats of arms stacked. The black horse in the middle comes from the city seal of Stuttgart — a town founded as a stud farm, hence the name. The antlers and red-and-black stripes around it come from the arms of Württemberg, the surrounding state.

Nothing on a Porsche badge refers to a car, an engine, speed, or the company. It is pure geography. It says: I am from a specific place, that place is old, and the engine is behind you.

Maserati — a fountain

Six Maserati brothers, five of them engineers. The sixth, Mario, was an artist, and when the family needed a badge he gave them the trident from the statue of Neptune in the Fountain of Neptune in Bologna's Piazza Maggiore.

It is a sea god's weapon, taken from a public fountain, applied to a car company. It makes no practical sense whatsoever and total emotional sense, which is the most Maserati sentence available.

Peugeot — the lion was on saw blades first

Peugeot's lion predates Peugeot's cars by about half a century. The company made steel goods — saw blades, coffee grinders, corset stays, tools — and adopted a lion around 1850, formally registering it in 1858. The qualities it was chosen to advertise were the qualities of a good saw: the flexibility of the blade, the speed of the cut, the strength of the tooth.

It did not appear on an automobile until around 1907. So Peugeot's badge does not mean French flair. It means: we made very sharp things before we made hatchbacks.

Rolls-Royce — a secret, in silver

The Spirit of Ecstasy has been leaning into the wind since 1911. Sculptor Charles Sykes modeled her, and she is widely associated with Eleanor Thornton, secretary to and secret partner of Lord Montagu of Beaulieu — a relationship the period could not accommodate publicly.

Before the Spirit of Ecstasy, Sykes had made Montagu a private mascot called The Whisper: the same woman, one finger pressed to her lips. Read that, then look at the ornament on the front of a Phantom again. She is not merely elegant. She is keeping something from you.

Volvo — the oldest symbol on any car

Volvo's badge is a circle with an arrow pointing up and to the right, and it is by some distance the most ancient mark in the industry. It is the alchemical and chemical symbol for iron, which is also the astronomical symbol for Mars, which is also the classical symbol for the god of war.

Volvo chose it in the 1920s to point at Swedish steel — the quality of the material the cars were made from. And the diagonal bar across the grille that the badge sits on was not styling. It started as the bracket holding the thing on.

The name is the same kind of joke as Audi's. Volvo is Latin: "I roll."

Part sixSaab, and a griffin that was never really theirs

Saab deserves its own section, because the story of its badge is a small tragedy about who owns a symbol.

Saab began as Svenska Aeroplan AB — a Swedish aircraft manufacturer that started building cars in 1945 essentially because the war had ended and the factories needed something to do. Its early car badges were what you would expect from an aeroplane company: an aeroplane.

The griffin came from somewhere else entirely. It is the coat of arms of Skåne — Scania — the southernmost province of Sweden, and it had been used since early in the twentieth century by Scania-Vabis, the truck maker based there. In 1969, Saab merged with Scania-Vabis to form Saab-Scania, and the two companies now shared a heraldic animal.

Even then it took fifteen years to reach the cars. The crowned red griffin's head that people picture when they think "Saab" was drawn by the artist Carl Fredrik Reuterswärd and did not appear on Saab automobiles until 1984.

Then it unravelled. General Motors bought into Saab in 1989 and its right to use the griffin ran on a clock, because Scania — the truck company, which still exists and is doing fine — was protective of its arms. The griffin came off Saab cars around 2010. When NEVS bought Saab's automotive assets in 2012 the deal gave them the name Saab but not the animal.

Saab's most recognizable symbol was a truck company's provincial coat of arms, arrived late, and was repossessed. And it still means Saab to everyone who ever loved one.The griffin, 1984–2010

There is a poignant footnote. Victor Muller, running Saab in its final years, said he had wanted to drop the griffin and go back to the aeroplane — on the grounds that the griffin belonged to Scania and Saab AB and "did not say anything." He was arguably right on the law and completely wrong about the feeling. Symbols do not care who holds the trademark.

One widely copied error

A great many sites state that the Saab griffin is "the coat of arms of the Count von Skåne." There is no Count von Skåne. Skåne is a province, and the griffin is its provincial coat of arms. The phrase appears to have been copied from one source into dozens without anyone stopping to ask who the count was.

Which is a fine illustration of how badge folklore propagates — the same way BMW got its propeller.

Part sevenThe flattening

Something has happened to badges in the last decade, and it is worth naming.

Between roughly 2019 and 2024, an extraordinary number of manufacturers redrew their emblems in almost exactly the same direction: chrome removed, gradients removed, three-dimensional modeling removed, everything reduced to a flat outline that renders cleanly at 24 pixels on a phone. Volkswagen, BMW, Nissan, Kia, Peugeot, Renault, Citroën, Cadillac, Aston Martin, Jaguar — a stampede.

The reasoning is not stupid. Badges now live mostly on screens, inside apps, as favicons, in app stores, at sizes where a chrome bevel turns to mud. A flat mark survives that. A sculpted one does not.

But something real is being traded away. A Porsche crest is a physical object with depth, enamel and gold. A flattened outline of a Porsche crest is a diagram of one. The badge stops being a piece of jewelry bolted to a machine and becomes an icon in a grid — and icons in grids all start to look like each other, which is precisely the problem a badge exists to solve.

The counter-argument is sitting in Part five. The marks that flatten best are the ones that were always simple: Citroën's chevrons, Volvo's iron symbol, Audi's rings, Mitsubishi's three diamonds. They lose nothing, because they never depended on rendering. They depended on meaning.

Part eightWhy the simple ones are usually the deepest

Which brings us back to where this started.

The badges that look like they mean the least almost always mean the most. An animal is a claim — anyone can claim to be fast. A crest is inherited — impressive, but nothing to do with the cars. Merger arithmetic is honest but tells you about a boardroom.

Whereas Volvo's badge is the symbol for iron, because Volvo was about the steel. Mitsubishi's three diamonds are two family crests fused, because that is literally what the company was. And Citroën's two little arrows are not arrows at all — they are a herringbone gear tooth, mirrored so that its forces cancel, which was André Citroën's first business, and which is also a perfect description of how that company has approached every engineering problem for a hundred years.

It is the most honest badge in the industry and it looks like a road sign. That story is here.

The verdict

An emblem is the only form of writing that can be read at sixty miles an hour by someone who is not paying attention, in a language they never studied, and still deliver a paragraph. It costs a second to receive and decades to earn — and the ones worth loving are never the ones that tried hardest to look meaningful. They are the ones that were already true, and simply got put on the nose of the car.

Fact-check notes

The 62–90% color figure comes from Singh (2006) and is real, but it describes snap assessment in controlled conditions, not purchase decisions. It gets stretched constantly. We have quoted it as what it is.

Color psychology generally. The robust finding is fit and appropriateness, not fixed universal meanings. Any source telling you a color simply "means" something is over-claiming.

Harry Sherman and 1914. The surgeon-switched-to-green story is well attested and repeated in medical literature including a 2009 CMAJ piece, but the attribution to a single named individual is traditional rather than rigorously documented.

Red stimulates appetite. Frequently asserted, weakly evidenced. Visibility at distance and arousal are the better-supported explanations for fast-food palettes.

The BMW propeller. Not the origin. Confirmed by BMW's own historians. The 1929 advertisement is the source of the myth.

Baracca's horse. The countess-suggested-it account is Ferrari's own telling and is the standard version. As with most founder stories, the details have been smoothed by repetition.

Saab. No Count von Skåne exists — see Part six.
