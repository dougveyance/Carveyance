Three Rotors, One Engine, No Exceptions — Mazda's Eunos Cosmo

The only three-rotor road car ever built, the world's first GPS touchscreen, and the worst launch timing in automotive history — all in one two-door. It arrived early, in a tuxedo, to a party that had already been canceled.

Carveyance  /  Eunos Cosmo (JC)  /  2026

Every so often, an engineering department gets handed the company checkbook, the keys to the lab, and a memo with exactly two words on it: impress everyone. Most of the time, sanity intervenes before the car reaches a showroom. In 1990, at Mazda, it didn't. The result was the Eunos Cosmo, and if you have never heard of it, that is precisely the tragedy this article exists to correct.

Picture the boldest idea Mazda's engineers had ever chased — a production car built around three spinning rotors instead of two, a configuration nobody else on Earth had ever dared to sell to a customer — and then picture that idea wrapped in leather, timber, and a touchscreen navigation system a full decade before anyone else thought drivers were ready for one. That is not hyperbole. That is the actual car. Officially it was the fourth and final generation of Mazda's storied Cosmo line, internally coded JC, built from April 1990 to 1995 as a big, rear-drive luxury coupe with a twin-rotor or, for the truly incurable, a three-rotor engine — both sequentially twin-turbocharged, both bolted to a four-speed automatic.

This was Mazda's moonshot: proof that Hiroshima could build something as plush as a Jaguar, as technologically unhinged as a concept car, and as mechanically exotic as anything wearing an Italian badge — all in one two-door. It gave the world the first series-production three-rotor road car and pioneered sequential twin-turbocharging inside that same engine. And it did every bit of this while being sold through a brand-new Japanese sales channel almost nobody understood, mere months before the economy that made the whole fantasy possible quietly folded itself into a shoebox.

The Eunos Cosmo did not just arrive ahead of its time. It arrived early, in a tuxedo, with champagne under one arm, to a party that had already been canceled.

First, a Small Matter of Identity

It's a Eunos, Not a Mazda

The car is most accurately called the Eunos Cosmo, not the Mazda Eunos Cosmo. Mazda created Eunos as one of several separate Japanese sales channels during its late-1980s expansion; in Japan the Cosmo was the flagship of the entire Eunos range — the halo car built to make customers stop thinking of Mazda as a maker of worthy family cars and start picturing it alongside Jaguar, BMW, and Mercedes-Benz.

Across the Pacific, Mazda was simultaneously cooking up a more explicit answer to Lexus and Acura called Amati, and the Cosmo was penciled in as one of Amati's launch models. That export dream evaporated when Amati was scrapped in October 1992 — after Mazda had already burned through roughly three years and $434 million developing the brand — killed by a collapsing Japanese economy before a single Amati showroom ever opened. The Cosmo stayed locked inside Japan's borders despite real curiosity from the British and Australian press, who had heard the rumors and wanted a look.

And here is the part that should genuinely surprise you: none of this was priced like lunacy. At home, the Cosmo opened around ¥3.3 million for the 13B Type-S and topped out near ¥5.3 million for the fully loaded 20B Type-E with its CCS electronics — roughly $23,000 to $37,000 at the exchange rates of the day. That undercut a BMW 850i, a Mercedes 500SEC, and a Porsche 928 by a serious margin. Whatever killed the Cosmo, it was never the number on the window sticker. It was everything wrapped around that sticker: an unfamiliar badge, a country you had to fly to just to see one in person, and a launch date that could not have landed on a worse page of the calendar.

Design

The Shape of Restrained Extravagance

Here is the first surprise about the Cosmo: the mechanical specification screams, but the body barely raises its voice. Stretching nearly sixteen feet from nose to tail on a wheelbase you could genuinely pitch a tent across, it carries classic grand-touring proportions — an impossibly long hood, a low roofline, real distance between the front axle and the windshield, and a short, gently sloping tail. There are no wings, no gaping vents, no motorsport costume jewelry anywhere on it. The design traces back to Mazda's MX-03 concept of 1985, and you can still feel the show-car discipline in the production shape: clean nose, slim lamps, narrow grille, frameless glass, understated brightwork.

To an enthusiast who already knows what is hiding under that hood, the restraint is delicious. To a wealthy shopper strolling past a prestige dealership in 1990, it may have been a genuine liability — because nothing about the outside of this car announces that it is wrapped around one of the most sophisticated powertrains ever bolted into a production automobile. Parked next to a BMW 850i, a Jaguar XJS, or a Mercedes SL, the Cosmo could disappear into the background entirely. That anonymity has aged into something wonderful. Today it looks like an artifact smuggled in from a parallel 1990s, one where optimism never ran out, touchscreens stayed thrilling, and nobody ever had to do math at the gas pump.

The Cabin

1990 Imagines the Twenty-First Century

Pull the enormous door shut and the Cosmo starts explaining itself immediately. The cabin does not offer a normal collection of gauges and switches — it wraps around you in one continuous horizontal sweep. Key off, most of the dashboard sits dark and silent. Key on, and the instrumentation rises out of that black panel like the command deck of a small, beautifully upholstered spacecraft powering up for departure. Mazda leaned all the way into the name, floating its displays against a midnight background decades before every new car learned to perform a little animated cabaret the instant you press the starter.

Material quality mattered just as much as the theater. Type-E models arrived swaddled in leather, with timber trim reportedly sourced from French elm and finished in Italy. And then there is the CCS — the Car Control System fitted to the range-topping Type-E: a color touchscreen that folded a full GPS navigation system, a television tuner, an optional integrated mobile telephone, a CD changer, and automatic climate control into one glowing interface, all of it operable from steering-wheel buttons if you preferred not to reach.

Here's the detail your history books usually skip: Mazda didn't build the CCS. Mitsubishi Electric did — a company that had gotten into satellite navigation by studying how it was used on Antarctic expeditions — partnering with Zenrin, a Japanese tourist-map publisher then in the middle of digitizing its maps. They launched it with the Cosmo in April 1990. And a necessary correction to the record while we're here: the Cosmo was not the first car with a touchscreen of any kind — that was the 1986 Buick Riviera, whose screen customers disliked and GM dropped by 1990. What the Cosmo actually pioneered is sharper and more important: it was the first production car anywhere to pair GPS navigation with a full-color touchscreen, tying navigation, entertainment, telephone, and climate under one interface. That is the recognizable great-grandparent of the screen glowing in whatever you drove here today.

Streets rendered slowly, the view was a flat 2D bird's-eye only, and the location marker was accurate to within about fifty meters. It ran on a cathode-ray tube — the same technology as your grandmother's television. It was also a touchscreen, in 1990, drawing the map you were driving across.

On the CCS, per Japanese Nostalgic Car

Was it fast? Not exactly — period reviewers reportedly clocked a single map zoom at nine full seconds, long enough by modern standards to reconsider your destination and write home about it. Judged against 1990, though, it wasn't simply ahead of the curve. It was out there drawing the curve freehand.

None of this came free of compromise, and the compromise is the one every genuine 2+2 coupe eventually signs. Front occupants sit low in broad, deeply supportive seats. The back seat offers decent width and headroom and almost no legroom whatsoever — call it occasional-use seating for adults, genuine seating for children, or, most honestly, exceptionally well-upholstered luggage space.

The Rotary Heart

Choose Your Number of Miracles

Two engines were on offer, though only one of them turned the Cosmo into a legend.

13B-RE: the twin-rotor

The smaller engine displaced 1,308cc across two 654cc chambers, breathing through fuel injection, intercooling, and sequential twin turbochargers for 230 PS at 6,500 rpm and 217 lb-ft arriving around 3,500 rpm. Call it the entry point rather than the afterthought — still an exceptionally smooth, compact, advanced engine, and its lighter nose made it the marginally more agile of the two cars. It outsold its three-rotor sibling by a comfortable margin (roughly 60/40), which tells you how many buyers wanted the fantasy without the full commitment.

20B-REW: the three-rotor masterpiece

The 20B bolts on a third 654cc chamber for 1,962cc total, officially rated at 280 PS at 6,500 rpm and 297 lb-ft at 3,000 rpm, again with sequential twin turbos. Mazda calls the Cosmo the world's first mass-produced car with a three-rotor engine and sequential twin-turbocharging, and it remains, to this day, the only production road car ever sold with the 20B-REW — a distinction Mazda itself has quietly called the best rotary engine it ever built. That factory-quoted 280 PS was a ceiling imposed by the era's informal gentleman's agreement among Japanese manufacturers, not the engine's true limit; independent period estimates commonly land closer to 295–320 real horsepower, which should be read as educated guesses rather than certified fact.

The sequential setup used one turbo for immediate low-speed response, then woke the second around 3,500 rpm, handing the rotary something most turbocharged engines of that decade simply did not have: real, usable torque well before the tachometer got interesting. But the bigger transformation is what that third rotor does to the engine's personality. A two-rotor already feels smooth, urgent, faintly otherworldly. Add a rotor and the whole thing gains density and continuity, until power stops arriving as a string of individual combustion events and starts arriving like pressure quietly building inside a turbine.

Mazda never built a V12. It built its own answer to the question a V12 asks.

Transmission & Chassis

The One Genuinely Frustrating Decision

Every single Cosmo, regardless of engine, used a four-speed electronically controlled automatic — not because Mazda forgot how to build a manual, but because this was conceived from day one as a luxury grand tourer, full stop. The gearbox offered Normal and Power modes plus a Hold function; the 20B ran taller gearing on a 3.909:1 final drive. Judged by 1990 standards it was smart and unobtrusive. Judged today, four ratios simply cannot keep up with an engine this good — the gaps blunt its edge, kickdown negotiates rather than reacts, and it is the transmission, not the rotary, quietly capping the whole experience around 7,000 rpm.

The chassis, by contrast, was pure indulgence: exclusive to the Cosmo, shared with nothing else Mazda ever built, running double-wishbone front suspension and a multilink rear with four dampers instead of the usual two, the extra pair mounted forward purely to sharpen ride control. Mazda actually considered four-wheel steering, all-wheel drive, and active suspension for this car and left all three out to manage weight and cost — a genuinely funny piece of restraint to find in a machine already carrying three rotors, twin turbos, GPS, and enough onboard wiring to run a regional airport. Four-wheel ventilated discs, ABS, and a viscous limited-slip diff came standard on 20B cars; the sportier Type-S added firmer springs and stickier rubber.

Behind the Wheel

What It Feels Like to Drive

Twist the key on a 20B and the Cosmo does not erupt. It gathers itself. The starter lets out a turbine-like whirr, then settles into a low, almost distant thrum, with none of the shiver you'd expect from a big piston engine and none of the throat-clearing drama of a V12 waking up. The rotary just begins spinning, calmly, as though it had been turning quietly before you ever climbed in.

Pull away and the first thing that hits you is refinement — light controls, an automatic that slides through its ratios without announcing itself, a cabin so hushed that wind and tire noise are more audible than the engine. Lean into the throttle and the first turbo answers instantly, with none of the flat-footed hesitation you might brace for. Then the second turbo wakes. It is not a violent kick so much as a second lung opening somewhere beneath that enormous hood — the scenery starts arriving noticeably faster, the turbine howl sharpens, and the Cosmo surges forward with an eerie total absence of vibration. It does not punch each gear into your ribs. It pours speed over the car, the way a tide comes in rather than the way a wave breaks — closer to being reeled toward the horizon than fired at it. Mazda quoted roughly 6.5 seconds to 60 mph and 14.1–14.5 through the quarter — numbers that wouldn't turn heads today, though the manner in which the Cosmo produces them still absolutely does.

The people who actually got the keys tended to reach the same conclusion.

“Not only is the Eunos Cosmo Mazda's magnum opus of the early 1990s, but simply one of the most technologically advanced cars of the era… the drivetrain hums with utmost smoothness. What a graceful creature.”

In the city

Around town the Cosmo is far more civilized than its résumé suggests it has any right to be — strong low-speed response, an automatic that never argues, and enough sound insulation to make traffic feel like somebody else's problem. What it is not is easy to place: broad, long, extremities out of view, limited rear visibility, and a 5.6-meter turning circle that does you zero favors on a tight street. Stop-and-go is also where its appetite hurts most, turning the fuel gauge into a clock with unusually expensive hands. And that dazzling touchscreen, when it survives at all this many decades later, tends to run slow, resist repair, and speak a navigation language the modern world has mostly forgotten — with the added indignity that because the CCS also runs the climate system, losing it costs you a lot more than just the radio.

On a twisting backroad

Adjust your expectations, because the résumé lies a little. Rear-drive, a violent engine, a name with real sporting pedigree, tuners who later treated this powertrain like scripture — surely it drives like an RX-7 wearing a smoking jacket? It does not. Turn into a tight corner and the long body needs a beat to settle, with real, noticeable lean, especially in the softer Type-E. The steering is accurate without being especially chatty; the car grips gamely and holds its line, but its size and wheelbase are never something you forget mid-corner. The 20B can absolutely overwhelm the rear tires, and the limited-slip diff rewards you with real drive out of a corner — but that four-speed automatic makes precise throttle management a negotiation rather than an instinct, and period testers weren't shy about brakes that felt outmatched by the performance on tap. Give it a wide, flowing road instead, feed it smooth inputs, and let the chassis take its time settling — the Cosmo sweeps rather than darts. Ask it to play RX-7 and you will both end the day frustrated.

On the motorway

Here, everything clicks. A fast, open highway is the Cosmo's private solar system — the long wheelbase settles the body, the suspension irons out anything the road throws at it, and the cabin isolates its occupants without ever fully cutting them off. The engine stays nearly silent until you actually ask something of it, at which point overtaking becomes one long, uninterrupted exhale. At an unrestricted top end estimated around 158–162 mph (Japanese cars were electronically capped near 115), the 20B had legitimately enough leg speed to run with serious European hardware. This is where the Cosmo stops being merely good and starts feeling correct: covering real distance at real speed, dashboard glowing, engine barely working.

The Field

The Competition It Walked Into Uninvited

Before meeting the specific cars, sit with this: none of them were target practice. This was Mazda — a company the world still filed under “makes decent little sports cars and honest family sedans” — walking uninvited into a room stacked with some of the most obsessively engineered grand tourers on the planet, and quietly asking to be taken seriously. A Jaguar V12 carried sixty years of Le Mans glory in its exhaust note before it ever turned a wheel for you. The Porsche 928 came from a company built entirely around performance engineering. And into that room walked a car wearing a brand-new badge nobody had heard of, sold in exactly one country, built around an engine configuration that existed in exactly one production car, ever, anywhere. That is not brave. That borders on unreasonable. And remarkably, in period road tests against this exact company, it held its own on merit alone.

Direct Japanese rivals

The Toyota Soarer 4.0 GT-Limited — and its export twin the Lexus SC 400 — was the closest thing to a direct hit: V8 power, deep brand familiarity, ambitious electronics, and the Lexus badge adding a prestige network built from scratch. The Nissan Leopard offered another tech-drenched personal-luxury coupe. The Acura Legend Coupe came from the front-drive, more conservative end but was beautifully screwed together. And the Nissan 300ZX Twin Turbo — one of the most adored sports cars in the world at that exact moment — brought a manual and sharper sporting credentials, trading away the Cosmo's handcrafted-lounge atmosphere for something rawer.

International targets

The Jaguar XJS V12 was the spiritual benchmark: effortless high-speed touring, twelve-cylinder smoothness, old-money racing glory. The BMW 850i was Munich's technological flagship, V12 power with genuine autobahn authority — more globally recognized but heavier and pricier. Mercedes' 500SEC and 500SL represented the establishment itself. The Porsche 928 brought a V8, a transaxle, and honestly superior circuit manners. And the Mitsubishi GTO (3000GT abroad) threw all-wheel drive, four-wheel steering, and active aero at the same problem — where the GTO used its gadgets to dominate the road, the Cosmo used its own to make the road feel optional.

The Ledger

The Benefits — and the Disappointments

What it got right

One of the smoothest, most genuinely distinctive production engines ever built; the only production application of the three-rotor 20B-REW that will ever exist; and real, seamless, high-speed grand-touring ability wrapped in a cabin that still feels startlingly quiet and rich decades later. Add the historically important GPS-and-touchscreen integration, a dedicated rear-drive chassis shared with nothing else Mazda built, and the plain fact of being rarer and more fascinating than nearly every rival on this list. It is neither a conventional luxury coupe nor a traditional rotary sports car — it is Mazda translating V12 grand touring into a language entirely its own, built before any focus group got to ask whether the business case worked.

Where it came up short

The same ambition that makes it special is what makes it flawed. No manual was ever offered, and the four-speed restricts both driver involvement and the engine's flexibility; handling stays secure without becoming genuinely agile; braking never quite matches the speed on tap. Fuel consumption ranges from poor to nearly satirical under boost. The rear seats are legroom-free and largely ceremonial. The CCS screens and cabin electronics are failure-prone and brutally hard to source parts for today. And the Japan-only sales left it without any global service network. The 20B also demands real mechanical respect — turbocharged rotaries despise detonation, overheating, poor lubrication, and careless tuning, and a neglected example can turn a rare masterpiece into an exceptionally decorative mortgage payment in a hurry.

The Bubble

Was It a Failure?

Commercially, yes. Creatively, not even close. At a production run of 8,875 cars, an exclusive platform, a dedicated body, two exotic engines, and a vast electronics program were never going to recoup their cost. One Japanese retrospective went so far as to brand it a “running bad debt” once the bubble burst — harsh, but not exactly wrong. Still, low sales don't mean Mazda built the wrong car. They mean Mazda built an extraordinarily expensive car for a market whose underlying assumptions collapsed almost the moment it reached showrooms. The Timing

Launched at Exactly the Wrong Moment

The Cosmo was developed squarely inside Japan's asset-price bubble, when soaring property and stock values convinced manufacturers that wealthy buyers would keep funding increasingly niche, elaborate products forever. Japanese companies responded by spinning up new brands, new dealer networks, exclusive platforms, and exotic engines aimed at vanishingly small audiences — and the bubble began collapsing at almost the exact moment the Cosmo hit the market. Luxury spending dried up, Mazda's ambitious five-channel dealership strategy turned into a financial anchor, and Amati was axed in 1992 before the Cosmo ever got its export shot.

It arrived in April 1990 carrying all the confidence of a decade that had just ended. Launch it in 1987 instead, and it might have enjoyed several genuinely exuberant years before the recession caught up. Launch it a decade later, and its navigation tech might have finally made sense to a market that had caught up — except emissions rules, fuel-economy pressure, and Mazda's own shaky finances would almost certainly have kept the 20B from ever existing at all. The timing is what killed the car. The timing is also exactly what created it.

There's a poignant footnote here: in fall 1991 Mazda issued a Type-SX special edition to celebrate its 1991 Le Mans victory — the 787B, the only Japanese car ever to win outright, powered by a four-rotor engine. Painted in Black Forest Mica on BBS wheels, it tied the road car to the greatest moment in rotary history at the precise moment the money was draining out of the room. Mazda's skunkworks even proposed a more upscale variant, the M2 1011. It was never built. There wasn't a market left to build it for.

The Verdict

You Do Not Attack the Road in a Cosmo. You Depart in It.

The Eunos Cosmo was a financial failure in exactly the way Concorde was a financial failure: the arithmetic never adds up, but staring only at the arithmetic misses the entire reason people still care. Mazda set out to prove that luxury did not require copying Mercedes, that refinement did not require pistons, and that a Japanese coupe could carry technological imagination equal to anything else on the planet. It succeeded.

This was never the finest sports car Mazda ever built — the RX-7 is lighter, sharper, and far more talkative through the wheel. It was never the safest business decision either. But the Cosmo remains the fullest expression of Mazda's willingness to be genuinely, defiantly different, and its true home was never the track, where its brakes and its automatic eventually wave the white flag, nor the tightest mountain road, where its size becomes the loudest thing in the car. It belongs on an open, sweeping road after dark: dashboard glowing across the cabin, first turbo laying down the momentum, second turbo reeling the horizon in closer, three rotors turning without any apparent effort while the long body settles into its stride.

You do not attack the road in a Cosmo. You depart in it. And somewhere between the turbine whisper, the glowing instruments, and the faintly comic sprint of the fuel gauge, you understand exactly what Mazda was trying to say: we can build a luxury car, we can build a technological masterpiece, and we can build something no other manufacturer on Earth would dare attempt. The tragedy was never that Mazda failed to make its point. The tragedy is that the world had stopped spending the very moment it finally started listening.
