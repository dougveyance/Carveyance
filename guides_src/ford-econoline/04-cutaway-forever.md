When van production ended in June 2014, the E-Series did something almost no retired vehicle does: it kept its job. Upfitters building box trucks, shuttle buses, ambulances and Class C motorhomes wanted a body-on-frame chassis with 40 years of tooling, training and parts behind it, so Ford kept building E-350 and E-450 cutaways and stripped chassis at Ohio Assembly. The V10 soldiered on, a 6.2 V8 appeared, and since 2021 the only engine is the 7.3-liter Godzilla pushrod V8 — 300 hp and 425 lb-ft standard, 350 and 468 in the premium calibration, CNG- and propane-ready. For 2026 it even got a new face, its first in nearly 20 years.

The sales chart is the punchline: 45,063 in 2019, 42,957 in 2023 — a "discontinued" vehicle outselling entire car lines a decade after its own funeral, and the platform is now 34 years old. U-Haul boxes, hotel shuttles, your neighborhood ambulance and half the Class C motorhomes at any campground ride on it.

## Buying one

Used cutaways are bought as working machines, so judge the body builder as much as the chassis: a Knapheide or Morgan box with clean seams, a shuttle with maintained wheelchair gear, an RV house without water intrusion. Chassis-side, the gas engines are long-proven — V10 exhaust studs and spark plugs on earlier units, fluid history on the TorqShift — and GVWRs mean nothing here carries an EPA rating; budget 8–12 mpg loaded and be pleasantly surprised. Commercial duty means commercial miles, so maintenance paper beats the odometer, same as every van in this guide.

And if you're speccing a new one for a camper or a work body, take a moment to appreciate the absurdity: in 2026 you can still order, brand new, the direct descendant of a 1975 Econoline — with a giant pushrod V8. The flat-nose Falcon van's great-grandchild outlived the minivan boom, the SUV boom and its own replacement. Some tools are simply finished designs.
