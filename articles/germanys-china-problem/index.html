Germany Built Its Car Industry on China. China Doesn't Need It Anymore.

German brands had about 26% of China's car market in 2019 and about 16% in 2025, and this year is worse. Why Volkswagen, Mercedes and Porsche are all losing the same market at once, and the 3 different ways they're answering.

By Camille Bouchard · 7 minute read

For most of the 2010s, the easiest money in the car business was a German car sold in China. Volkswagen sold more than 4 million cars a year there. Mercedes, BMW and Porsche sold their most expensive models to a fast-growing class of buyers who wanted exactly what they made. China paid for a lot of Germany's car industry, and Germany came to depend on it.

This week's results showed how far that has turned. Volkswagen Group's deliveries in China fell 38.5% in the third quarter, to 405,800, and 30.2% over the first 9 months. Mercedes's car sales there fell 31% in the quarter, and it cut its forecast. Porsche's China deliveries are down 33% this year, to 21,493. Volkswagen's share of its Chinese joint ventures' results was a loss of €113 million (about $127 million) over 9 months, against a profit of €744 million (about $833 million) a year earlier. "China is no longer a growth machine," Ralf Brandstätter, the head of Volkswagen's China business, told Handelsblatt, as translated by The Next Web.

What changed

The first change is the powertrain. New-energy vehicles, China's term for electric cars and plug-in hybrids, passed half of new-vehicle sales within China in 2025, according to the China Association of Automobile Manufacturers, and reached a record 67% of retail sales in September 2026, according to the China Passenger Car Association. The German brands built their reputation, and their margins, on engines. Chinese brands built theirs on batteries and software, and they had the market to themselves while it grew.

The second is price. On October 27, BYD launched a new Qin Plus DM-i, a plug-in hybrid sedan 4.84 meters (15.9 ft) long, from an introductory 76,900 yuan (about $11,500), with lidar-based driver assistance as an option. Its Seagull hatchback starts under $10,000, Electrek reported. That's the competition a German car now faces, at its own price and well below it. The third is what buyers think the badge means. "Maybe some younger customers perceive us as the brand for the parents," Robert Cisek, the head of the Volkswagen brand in China, told Reuters in April. Yale Zhang, of the consultancy Automotive Foresight, was blunter: "You can't really rely on your chrome metal strips, your Napa leather seats and your 'one-hundred-year' history."

And the market itself is getting smaller. Retail car sales in China fell 21.7% in the first 9 months of 2026, the China Passenger Car Association says. In a shrinking market, the brands losing share lose twice.

3 companies, 3 answers

Volkswagen is cutting, and borrowing. It's running a cost program of about €31 billion (about $34.7 billion), it reported a €5.9 billion (about $6.6 billion) operating loss for the third quarter after writing down Porsche and its Chinese assets, and it has paused work on the electric Golf. It's also studying whether to put CEA, the electronic architecture it developed with the Chinese EV maker Xpeng, under future Volkswagens, Skodas and Cupras in Europe, Reuters reported. For decades, Germany sent its technology to China. Volkswagen is now considering sending China's back to Germany.

Porsche is shrinking on purpose. It sold 16% fewer cars in the first 9 months, while its revenue fell only 5.9%, so its revenue per car delivered rose to about €141,600 (about $158,600), from about €126,400, by Carveyance's calculation from Porsche's figures. It chose price over volume. "Porsche remains committed to its value-over-volume strategy," said Matthias Becker, its head of sales and marketing. China's share of Porsche's deliveries has fallen to 12%, from 15%.

Mercedes is staying. "If you are a global carmaker like Mercedes, there is no strategy in which you can leave China aside," Ola Källenius, the chief executive, told analysts, as reported in Spanish by Híbridos y Eléctricos and translated here. Its answer is a China-specific electric E-Class in the second half of 2027, more production in Hungary, where Källenius says costs are about 70% lower than in Germany, and a push in America, where it wants to grow from about 300,000 cars a year to about 400,000.

Who pays for it

The bill lands in Germany. Volkswagen's plan already includes up to 100,000 job cuts in total, Reuters reported, and the Osnabrück plant is to be sold and converted to defense production. Mercedes is negotiating savings with its workers. Wage talks between Volkswagen and the IG Metall union restart on November 24, and the union's peace obligation ends on December 31. None of that was the plan 10 years ago, when China was where the growth came from.

The German brands aren't finished in China. Volkswagen still sold almost 2.7 million cars there last year, more than most carmakers sell anywhere. But the era when China paid for Germany's car industry is over, and the companies are now competing with Chinese brands in Europe too. The question is no longer how to win China back. It's how to build a profitable car company without it.

The short version

German brands' share of China's car market fell from about 26% in 2019 to about 16% in 2025, and this year Volkswagen, Mercedes and Porsche are all down about 30% there. The causes are the same for all 3: China went electric fast, Chinese brands are far cheaper and more advanced in software, and the market is shrinking. Volkswagen is cutting €31 billion and considering Chinese technology for Europe, Porsche is selling fewer cars for more money, and Mercedes says it can't leave China aside.
