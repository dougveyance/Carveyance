The Bronco Sport has spent much of its life being asked a question that misses the point: is it a real Bronco? It isn't a shrunken version of the body-on-frame, removable-door Bronco, and pretending otherwise would do it no favors. It's a useful, upright, 5-passenger crossover that trades a little urban smoothness for better trail manners and a remarkable amount of clever packaging. For plenty of buyers, that's the more relevant offer.

## The architecture behind the name

Ford started building the Bronco Sport in Hermosillo, Mexico, in October 2020, as a 2021 model. It's a unibody crossover on the same platform as the Escape, with 4-wheel drive standard, which Ford markets as 4x4. On the Base, Big Bend and Outer Banks, the system works much like the Escape's all-wheel drive, with its own programming for off-road use. The Badlands has more hardware, covered in its own chapter.

The roofline stays high and square, which makes for an unusually practical cargo area in a compact footprint: up to 65.2 cubic feet with the rear seat folded, in a 1.5-liter. The rear glass flips up on its own, so you can drop small things in without opening the whole liftgate. The cabin uses wipe-clean materials and has plenty of storage, although the hard plastics and wind noise remind you it isn't a small luxury SUV.

## The engine decision

Most Bronco Sports have a 1.5-liter turbo 3-cylinder, rated at 181 hp and 190 lb-ft for these years, with an 8-speed automatic. It handles daily driving capably and is the efficient choice: the EPA rates it at 26 mpg combined. The Badlands, and the 2021 First Edition, have a 2.0-liter turbo 4-cylinder with 245 hp and 275 lb-ft, and much stronger acceleration. It's rated at 23 mpg.

Car and Driver ran a 1.5-liter Outer Banks to 60 mph in 8.2 seconds and a 2.0-liter Badlands in 5.9. Don't assume a higher trim has the bigger engine: an Outer Banks has the 1.5.

## Road manners

Compared with the full Bronco, the Sport is easier to park and less theatrical at highway speed. Its short length and upright seating are pleasant in the city, though the ride can turn busy over harsh expansion joints, especially on the off-road versions. The 1.5 is fine for commuting but can sound strained when you push it. On loose surfaces, the traction hardware and drive modes take it farther than its neighbors in the parking lot would expect, but it isn't built for the articulation and abuse of a trail Bronco.

| 2024 | Last year before the update. Free Wheeling edition |

## What we would buy

For people who want the shape, the cargo space and weekend dirt-road confidence, a Big Bend on sensible tires is the compelling value. A well-equipped Outer Banks makes sense for long commutes, but it can cost enough that bigger crossovers enter the conversation. The Badlands is the enthusiast's pick, because its extra ability is mechanical, not cosmetic. And whichever you choose, a 1.5-liter from these years must have its fuel-injector recall done.
