The Prius Paradox

Toyota just had the biggest hybrid quarter in its history, and the car that made the hybrid famous is selling about half as many as last year. Why the Prius is losing to its own family.

By Elliot Vance · 6 minute read

For 25 years, the Prius was the hybrid. If you wanted one, you bought the car with the wedge shape and the blue badge, and everyone on the road knew what you had chosen. This year, Americans are buying more hybrid Toyotas than ever before. They just aren't buying the Prius.

Toyota's third-quarter results, released October 1, make the split plain. Electrified models, almost all of them hybrids, made up 57.4% of everything Toyota and Lexus sold in America from July through September: 363,367 vehicles, up 28.5% from a year earlier. Over the first 9 months of the year, sales of the regular Prius hybrid fell 51%.

The numbers

Toyota sold 16,731 regular Prius hybrids through September, against 34,237 in the same months of 2025. The plug-in version held steady at 12,492, against 12,540. In the third quarter alone, the 2 versions together came to 9,705, down 25%.

For scale: in September alone, Toyota sold 25,350 Camry Hybrids. That's more than all the regular Prius hybrids it sold in the first 9 months of the year.

Everything around the Prius grew. Toyota doesn't publish quarterly sales by model, so the figures below are worked out from its monthly reports.

How the Prius got its job

The Prius went on sale in the US in 2000, and for years it was the one Toyota designed from the start as a hybrid. Other models offered the system as an option; the Prius was built around it. Its family peaked in 2012, when Toyota sold 236,655 of the Prius, the smaller Prius c, the larger Prius v and the first plug-in together.

That was a car with a job. A buyer who wanted a hybrid chose a Prius, and the shape announced the choice. Over the next decade the job quietly went away.

Then most Toyotas became hybrids

Toyota started putting its hybrid system into its mainstream models, first as an option and then, model by model, as the only choice.

Not every Toyota has gone that way. The Tundra, Grand Highlander, Corolla, Corolla Cross, Tacoma and 4Runner are still sold with gasoline-only engines, and 42.6% of Toyota and Lexus sales in the quarter weren't electrified. But the RAV4 and the Camry, 2 of Toyota's best-sellers, now come only as hybrids. A buyer who wants a hybrid no longer has to choose a car built around being one.

The best Prius ever, at the worst time

The timing is what makes it a paradox. The current Prius, on sale since January 2023, is the best-reviewed in the car's history. It traded the wedge for a low, sleek fastback, it makes 194 hp with front-wheel drive and 196 hp with all-wheel drive, and the EPA rates the base front-drive model at 57 mpg combined. MotorTrend named it Car of the Year for 2024, the second time a Prius had won.

And for 2 years it sold. Prius sales, hybrid and plug-in together, rose from 38,052 in 2023 to 44,711 in 2024 and 56,488 in 2025, the most since 2018. The fall came this year.

What Toyota says

Toyota's explanation, given to The Drive in April after a 41.5% first-quarter drop, points at its own showroom. "We saw demand shift toward Camry, largely because of its strong fuel economy," Derrick Brown of Toyota Motor North America said. He added that the Camry and Prius share parts, which let Toyota build fewer of one and more of the other.

The 2 cars also come from different places. The Prius is built in Tsutsumi, Japan. The Camry is built in Georgetown, Kentucky. Toyota hasn't said that location played any part.

The plug-in tells the rest of the story. It's the one Prius that offers something the other hybrid Toyotas mostly don't, the ability to run on electricity alone for short trips, and its sales held flat. It now accounts for 43% of all Prius sales this year, up from 27% in 2025.

A car defined by its powertrain has a problem when that powertrain is everywhere.

A hybrid is now a choice, not a car

Hybrids reached a record 16% of US new-vehicle sales in the second quarter, according to the Energy Information Administration. Cox Automotive put the figure at 16.3% at midyear and says Toyota alone accounts for 44% of them. That's the market the Prius built, and it no longer needs the Prius to reach it.

That is the paradox in one line. The Prius won the argument it was built to make, and now the argument has moved into the Camry, the RAV4, the Corolla and the Sienna. Buyers can get hybrid fuel economy without choosing a car whose whole identity is being a hybrid, and most of them are doing exactly that.

Toyota is still selling the Prius, with a 2027 model priced from $28,755 before destination. On the EPA's 2026 ratings it is still the most efficient regular hybrid Toyota sells. It just isn't the only way anymore, and that was the job it used to have.

The short version

Toyota sold more hybrids than ever this summer, and regular Prius sales have fallen by half this year. The best Prius ever is losing to the Camry, RAV4 and Corolla hybrids that its own success created. The plug-in version, the one with something the others don't offer, is holding steady.
