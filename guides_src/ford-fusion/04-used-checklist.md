Around 3.2 million Fusions mean the used lots will never run out, so the right move is ruthlessness: check the specifics below, and walk from anything that fails — an identical car is a week away.

## The 5 checks

**1. Recalls, by VIN, first.** The 2013–14 1.6 EcoBoost cars carried campaigns tied to overheating, and various years drew door-latch and shifter-cable recalls — all free dealer fixes. An open recall on the report is a condition of sale, not a discount conversation.

**2. Transmission behavior, by era.** Early first-generation cars used Mazda-designed 5-speed autos: feel for flare and harsh 2–3 shifts warm. The 6F35 6-speed in later cars is stouter but still wants fluid history. On 2013–20 cars, light-throttle clunks or hunting deserve a second opinion before money moves.

**3. Rust, underneath.** The oldest Fusions are 20 years old now. Subframes, rear arches and brake lines on salt-state cars tell you more than the odometer does; a clean southern car is worth real premium.

**4. Turbo oil discipline.** Any EcoBoost — 1.5, 1.6, 2.0 or the Sport's 2.7 — forgives little oil neglect. Receipts for regular changes are the difference between a bargain and a bet, and on the Sport, confirm the all-wheel-drive system engages smoothly and the 19-inch tires wear evenly.

**5. Hybrid and Energi health.** Watch the battery gauge cycle on a full warm-up drive; intrusive engine drone at steady throttle is normal, warning lights are not. On Energis, test the charge port with the car's own cord and confirm electric range in the cluster roughly matches the era's 22–25 miles.

## Reading the market

The arc above is your leverage. The 2013–2017 flood keeps second-generation supply deep and prices soft; the post-announcement years thinned demand faster than supply, so even the last 2019–2020 cars trade at compact-car money. Nothing here appreciates, and nothing needs to — buy the best-kept Fusion in reach, drive it for years of cheap miles, and let the depreciation someone else already paid do the work.
