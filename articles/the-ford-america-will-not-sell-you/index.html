The Ford America Will Not Sell You

And the tariff math that finally decides it

Ford builds a body-on-frame, seven-seat, 7,700-pound-towing SUV on the same chassis as the Ranger and the Bronco. It won Australia’s car of the year. The French Army bought four thousand of them. You cannot have one — and the reason has changed.

By Oliver Bacon  ·  Opinions  ·  24 min read

In February of this year, a left-hand-drive Ford Everest was photographed driving around Detroit with no camouflage on it at all. Manufacturer plates. No trim badges. And, sticking out from under the rear bumper, a special exhaust tip of the kind fitted to a vehicle whose emissions are being measured.

Ford tests global-market vehicles in Michigan constantly, and the company has been consistent for four years that the Everest is not coming here. A single spy shot proves nothing. But it was the third sighting since 2021, the first without camouflage, and the emissions hardware is a detail that does not belong on a car being validated for Thailand. Two months later another one turned up. There are reports of a third in Colorado.

So it seems worth asking the question properly, because the answer is genuinely different than it was three years ago, and almost everyone arguing about it — on both sides — is arguing from the wrong premise.

01What it actually is

The Everest has existed since 2003. It has never been sold in the United States, which is why it reads as exotic to an American audience and reads as completely ordinary to about half the planet.

The first generation shared roughly sixty percent of its parts with the Ranger of the day and was sold across Southeast Asia, India, the Middle East, Central America, the Bahamas and much of Africa. In India it was called the Endeavour, because a spice company already owned the Everest name. In Indonesia you could order one with ten seats, the third row facing each other across the load bay like a troop carrier.

The second generation, from 2015, moved to Ford’s global T6 architecture and was engineered in Australia. That car went on to a second career nobody expected: the French Army adopted a militarized version as the Arquus Trapper VT4, and took delivery of more than four thousand of them, the last batch arriving in 2023. Set that beside the usual objection that the Everest is too niche for a serious market.

The current car arrived in 2022, developed in Australia again, on the T6.2 platform. Its platform siblings are the current Ranger, the current Volkswagen Amarok, and — this is the part Americans keep missing — the current Bronco. It won Wheels Car of the Year in 2022, the first Ford to take Australia’s biggest award since the Territory in 2004.

And it sells. Australia bought 26,494 in 2024 and 26,161 in 2025. Vietnam took 13,056 last year. This is not a curiosity built in a shed for enthusiasts to sigh over. It is a mainstream, high-volume, award-winning family SUV that happens to be invisible from North America.

02The hardware

Engines vary by market, which is itself part of the story. The hero is a 3.0-liter turbodiesel V6 making 247 horsepower and 443 lb-ft, through a ten-speed automatic — the same 10R80 in half the Fords in your local dealership. Below it sits a 2.0-liter four-cylinder diesel at 168 horsepower. Above and beside it, depending on where you live, are two gasoline engines: a 2.3-liter EcoBoost four making between 271 and 298 horsepower, and — hold this thought — a 2.7-liter EcoBoost V6 making 310 horsepower and 400 lb-ft, sold in the Middle East.

The suspension is where it separates from the pickup it shares a frame with. The Ranger has leaf springs at the back. The Everest has a coil-sprung live axle on a Watt’s link, with longer front control arms to suit a track widened by two inches over the previous car. That single change is why every reviewer who drives one says the same thing: it rides like a heavy wagon, not like a truck with seats in the bed.

The Tremor goes further with Bilstein position-sensitive dampers and a track widened again to 65 inches. New Zealand’s Driven, after a full test, called it capable, comfortable and effortless, and reckoned it the best in its class outright — conceding the Toyota Prado has the nicer cabin and arguing it cannot come close on value.

03The four-wheel-drive system nobody expects

Lower trims get a straightforward part-time setup: 2H, 4H, 4L, two-speed case, shift on the fly. Fine. Ordinary.

The higher trims get something better. Ford’s Advanced 4WD adds a fourth position, 4A, and it is not the marketing gimmick that name usually signals. The system uses an electromechanical transfer case with a multi-plate clutch pack, varying the split anywhere from pure rear-drive to a locked 50:50, defaulting to a nominal 40:60 rear bias. It sends torque forward under hard acceleration to kill wheelspin, and forward again if it senses the tail stepping out. It will shift from two-wheel drive to four at up to 87 mph.

Then, when you actually need it, 4H and 4L mechanically lock the clutch pack. This is the bit that matters. Most set-and-forget systems are permanently a clutch pack pretending to be a differential. This one is a proper locked four-wheel drive that happens to have a very good automatic mode bolted on top, with a rear locker behind it and underbody protection and two recovery hooks standard on every single Everest built.

One detail I have not stopped thinking about: the system stays locked when you switch the engine off, so the park pawl and the handbrake hold through all four wheels. That is a decision made by somebody who has parked on a slope in the wet.

The Ranger has leaf springs. The Everest has coils and a Watt’s link. Everything people like about driving one traces back to that.

04Why it isn’t here

Four reasons, in descending order of honesty.

Cannibalization, which is the real one

The Explorer sold 222,706 units in America in 2025, up twenty-six percent, and remains the best-selling three-row SUV in the country. Bronco had its strongest year since the revival. Ford has precisely zero commercial incentive to introduce a third mid-size SUV that takes bites out of both. Every other explanation is downstream of this one.

Capacity

Michigan Assembly in Wayne builds the Ranger and the Bronco. A third T6.2 product means slots taken from two profitable ones.

Powertrain certification

The diesels are not federalized, and certifying a 3.0 V6 diesel for EPA and CARB is a large bill for a market with almost no appetite for diesel SUVs.

The official line

Ford has said since 2022 that the Everest is for markets where the Bronco and Explorer are not sold. That is a real answer, and it was a good one.

05The argument that has quietly collapsed

Here is where nearly every version of this conversation goes wrong, including the enthusiast version. People say just import it. As of 2026 that is no longer a difficult proposition. It is a dead one, and the arithmetic is brutal.

SUVs escape the 25 percent chicken tax — that applies to pickups. It does not help nearly enough.

On a $45,000 vehicle that is roughly seventeen thousand dollars of duty before a dealer has touched it, against a Toyota 4Runner that starts at $43,565. There is no trim strategy, no content decontenting, no clever configuration that survives that. Importing the Everest is finished.

The part that cuts the other way Ford does not need to import it. T6.2 is already tooled in Wayne, Michigan for the Ranger and the Bronco. A domestically assembled Everest qualifies under USMCA at zero percent and is eligible for the domestic-assembly offset. The tariff argument does not kill the Everest in America. It kills exactly one method of getting it here, and that method was never the sensible one.

06The engine is already sitting on the shelf

The diesel-certification objection dissolves the moment you look at what Ford is already building.

The Middle Eastern Everest Tremor does not use the 3.0 diesel. It uses the 2.7-liter EcoBoost V6 — the engine from the F-150 and the North American Ranger — making 310 horsepower and 400 lb-ft through the same ten-speed. A gasoline, US-certified, already-in-production Ford engine, already installed in an Everest, already validated, already selling.

Nobody has to develop anything. The hard engineering was finished for a market of a few thousand cars a year in the Gulf.

The powertrain problem was solved two years ago, for the Middle East, by accident.

07Where it would land, and against whom

Toyota is currently running the experiment for Ford and publishing the results. 4Runner sales are up 91.4 percent in 2026, at 85,677 units through July, and Toyota cannot build them quickly enough. The Land Cruiser starts at $58,080. There is demonstrable, unmet, growing American demand for a body-on-frame mid-size SUV that is not a Wrangler competitor.

Against the 4Runner the Everest wins on towing by nearly two tons, on the third row being usable rather than punitive, and on having a transfer case that does both jobs. It loses on resale value, on dealer familiarity, and on the fact that the 4Runner is not a vehicle so much as a cultural institution with a waiting list.

Against the Bronco it is the one you take to Moab and then to your in-laws without changing anything. Against the Explorer it is slower, thirstier and less refined, and tows two thousand pounds more.

My estimate: it would sell somewhere between 40,000 and 60,000 units a year. Real money. Roughly a quarter of the Explorer’s volume.

Which is exactly the problem. Ford does not need a product that sells 50,000 units if 20,000 of those buyers walked in intending to buy an Explorer. The Everest’s case in America has never been about whether Americans would buy it. Of course they would. It is about whether Ford would rather sell you an Everest at a mid-size margin or an Explorer at a three-row-crossover margin, and that question answers itself in about four seconds.

08Three Fords, three completely different ideas

The objection that the Everest would simply eat the Bronco and the Explorer only works if you think all three are the same kind of vehicle. Spend five minutes with what each one actually is and the overlap gets much smaller than the spreadsheet suggests.

The Bronco is a toy, and that is a compliment

The Bronco’s entire reason for existing is that the roof and the doors come off. It is a Wrangler competitor and it competes on Wrangler terms: 300 horsepower from the 2.3 EcoBoost or 330 from the 2.7 V6, seven trims, two- or four-door, and a Sasquatch package with 35-inch tires on beadlock-capable wheels, front and rear electronic lockers, a 4.7 final drive and a disconnecting front sway bar. Up to 13.1 inches of ground clearance, 37 inches of fording, and a 43.2-degree approach angle in Raptor form.

What it is not is a family hauler. Five seats maximum. Towing tops out at 3,500 pounds, or 4,500 with the V6. Open-top vehicles are noisy, they leak wind, and the cargo area is compromised by the hardware that makes the roof removable. The Bronco is bought by people who want a vehicle that is an event. That is a real and enormous market, and it is not the same market as somebody with a roof tent, two kids and a trailer.

The Explorer is a very good crossover wearing outdoor clothing

The 2026 Explorer is unibody, rear-drive-based, and priced from about $40,160 to roughly $56,600. Two engines: the 2.3 EcoBoost four at 300 horsepower, and a 3.0 twin-turbo V6 at 400 that will run an ST to sixty in about 5.1 seconds. Six or seven seats. A 13.2-inch screen with Google built in. BlueCruise hands-free driving.

For 2026 the Timberline gave way to a Tremor trim — Intelligent 4WD, a Torsen limited-slip rear, all-terrain tires, skid plates and 8.7 inches of ground clearance. Which sounds like an Everest until you notice the numbers: an inch and a half less clearance than a base Everest, no low range, no rear locker, and 5,000 pounds of towing, or 5,600 with the V6.

That is not a criticism. The Explorer is quicker, quieter, better inside and far nicer on a long interstate run than any ladder-frame SUV, and it sells 222,000 units a year because that is what most three-row buyers actually want. But an Explorer Tremor is a crossover with good tires. It is an impression of the thing the Everest simply is.

The Everest is the one that goes to work

Ladder frame. Low range. Rear locker on every version built. Ten inches of clearance and 31.5 of wading in Tremor form. Two recovery hooks bolted to the front of the chassis as standard equipment, which is a statement about expected use. Seven seats. And 7,716 pounds of towing — 2,100 more than the best Explorer and more than double a base Bronco.

Three ethos, three buyers. The Bronco sells the weekend. The Explorer sells the school run with a hint of adventure. The Everest sells the thing neither of them does: a vehicle you load with seven people and a camper and a week of gear, drive four hundred miles of interstate in comfort, then take up a fire road in low range without a moment’s anxiety, and repeat every summer for fifteen years.

The Bronco takes the roof off. The Explorer takes the interstate. The Everest takes the trailer, the family and the fire road, in one trip.

Does some overlap exist? Of course. A Bronco four-door with Sasquatch and an Everest Tremor will attract a few of the same people, and an Explorer Tremor buyer might be tempted. But the honest number is smaller than Ford’s planners fear, and the segment it would actually raid is the one Ford does not currently compete in at all — the 4Runner’s.

09Can you just buy one?

No. And the version of “yes” you have read on forums is wrong in a way that can cost you the car and a great deal more than the car.

Two clocks, not one

The famous twenty-five-year rule is the DOT and NHTSA safety exemption, and it runs from the vehicle’s actual month and year of manufacture — not its model year. A car built in November 2001 does not qualify until November 2026. Miss that by six weeks and you own a very large paperweight.

Emissions are a completely separate rulebook. The EPA exemption begins at twenty-one years, and only if the car keeps its original engine and original emissions configuration. So the two clocks can disagree in both directions: a twenty-two-year-old car can satisfy the EPA and remain blocked by DOT, and an engine-swapped twenty-five-year-old car can clear DOT and create a fresh EPA problem.

For the Everest, that means the first generation — which arrived in 2003 — starts becoming admissible in 2028, unit by unit, according to each individual car’s build date. And what you would be importing is a 2.5-liter diesel on leaf springs with no rear locker: a genuinely different vehicle from the one this article is about.

The Canada myth, and why it fails Canada admits foreign-market vehicles at fifteen years, which is why a 2011 Everest can be sitting legally on Ontario plates right now. It does not follow that you can then drive it south. The United States regulates the vehicle at the moment of US importation, not the country it last lived in. A Thai-built Everest registered in Canada is still a Thai-certified vehicle with a Canadian address — it has no manufacturer-applied FMVSS label, and Canada does not retroactively make Ford certify anything. At the border, CBP asks which US provision authorizes entry, and the answer is still the twenty-five-year clock. Canadian registration changes the car’s location and paperwork history. It changes nothing about its age, its original certification, or its federal admissibility.

The routes that do exist, and why none of them fit

Registered Importer conversion. NHTSA must first rule the model eligible, which requires either substantial similarity to a US-certified vehicle or proof it can be modified to meet every applicable standard. A Registered Importer then does and documents the work, against a DOT conformance bond of 150 percent of dutiable value. For a model never crash-tested to US standards, generating acceptable crashworthiness evidence is not expensive so much as impossible — you cannot cheaply modify your way past a destructive test for which no data exist.

Show or Display. Requires NHTSA approval before importation, applies to historically or technologically significant vehicles, and caps road use at roughly 2,500 miles a year. Being desirable and unavailable does not qualify a car. A seven-seat family SUV built in the tens of thousands annually is not what this provision is for.

The nonresident provision. A genuine visitor may temporarily drive a foreign-registered vehicle here, for personal use, for up to one year, without selling it, and NHTSA says that period cannot be extended. A US resident who forms a Canadian company, registers a truck to it and calls himself a visitor is not clever; CBP can examine actual residence, ownership, insurance, where the vehicle is garaged and how it is used. Bouncing across the border does not restart the clock on a car that plainly lives in Michigan.

And the thing people actually do — drive it over and hope — is not a loophole, it is unlawful importation. Refusal, seizure, forced export, forfeiture, storage costs, civil penalties, a state title you cannot obtain and an insurer who will not pay after a loss. The HS-7 form carries its own warning: a knowingly false declaration can mean a fine of up to $10,000, up to five years, or both. Calling it parts does not help. Neither does the VIN plate from something else, which converts a customs problem into a felony.

The honest summary

If you live in Canada, you can own a second-generation Everest today and enjoy it enormously. If you live in the United States, you wait — until 2028 at the earliest, for the oldest and least interesting version of this vehicle, one build date at a time. Everything else is a story someone tells at a car meet. The border is not persuadable, and it does not drink whiskey.

The verdict

The Everest belongs in Ford’s American lineup on every measure except the only one that counts. It fills a real hole — body-on-frame, three rows, 7,700 pounds of towing, priced under the Explorer and between the Broncos. The engine exists. The platform is already tooled in Michigan. The demand is visible in Toyota’s order books. Importing it is dead on arrival at thirty-eight percent duty, but nobody serious was proposing that anyway. The only genuine obstacle left is that Ford would have to be willing to take sales from itself, and no company has ever done that voluntarily. Which means the Everest will arrive here the day an outside competitor makes it more expensive not to — and not one day earlier.
