Putting a full-size van on a real truck frame sounds obvious now because the 1975 Econoline made it obvious. The VN chassis dropped the hood, simplified service, and handed ambulance, bus and RV builders a frame to bolt bodies onto — the move that made the E-Series infrastructure. Motor Trend handed it the first Truck of the Year award in 1978, buyers handed it the full-size-van crown in 1980, and the range swelled to meet them: 2 wheelbases, the stretched Super Van and Super Wagon, 15-seat Club Wagons, and engines from the immortal 300 six through the 302 and 351 to the 460 big-block, with Navistar's 6.9 and 7.3 IDI diesels arriving in 1983.

This is the van of 1980s America — the two-tone Chateau with captain's chairs, the church bus, the airport shuttle, the contractor's rig with a million miles of ladder rack. EPA ratings existed by now and tell the honest story: 14 combined for a 4.9-six E-150, 11 for the 5.8, and nothing at all for the heavy 350s, which sat above the rating line then just as their descendants do today.

## Buying one now

The 300 six is the engine to seek — gear-driven cam, farm-implement internals, essentially eternal — while the small V8s are fine and the 460 is for towing and fuel-card holders. The IDI diesels are slow but stout; just budget for injection-pump work and glow-plug relays. Rust is the whole inspection: floors, rockers, rear arches, the windshield surround, and the frame itself in salt states. Interiors are vinyl-tough, trim is the hard part to find, and drivetrain parts are still at the counter of any parts store in America.

Prices remain the bargain of the vintage-van world. Honest drivers come in around $5,000–$10,000, the nicest Chateaus and conversion survivors in the teens, and the nostalgia wave that already lifted square-body pickups is visibly forming behind these. Buy the cleanest original two-tone you can find and park it next to anything from 1985 — it's the one people will walk to.
