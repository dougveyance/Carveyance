Start where the work happened. Open the back and read the load floor: screw holes from shelving, gouges from pallets, paint worn to steel where cargo slid. None of it is disqualifying — these are work vans — but it calibrates the price and tells you whether "light use" is true. Cycle both sliding doors full-travel several times; rollers and tracks are the model's signature wear item in both generations, and a door that hesitates or crunches is your first negotiating line. In salt states, go over rockers, rear arches and the liftgate's lower seam, then look underneath at the rear beam and subframe. A lined, clean cargo bay with a thick service folder beats low miles every time.

Drivetrains sort by era. The gen-1 2.0 and 4-speed are crude and durable — confirm smooth shifts and no overheating history and move on. The 2014–21 2.5 is the same story with more power. The 1.6 EcoBoost wants proof of oil changes and a cold-start listen, plus a hard look at coolant level and any sweet smell inside. The 2019-on 2.0 GDI with the 8-speed is the modern one — just verify the transmission's low-speed manners on the test drive and that recalls are closed out. On Wagons, check every seat latch and fold; on ex-taxis and shuttles, assume double the wear the odometer admits.

## The deal

Price the configuration first, the condition second. Cargo vans are plentiful and cheapest; Wagons carry a family premium; EcoBoosts and Titaniums are thin on the ground and priced accordingly. Figure $4,000–$7,000 for a sound gen-1, low-to-mid teens for a tidy mid-2010s cargo van, and high teens to low $20,000s for the best late 2.0/8-speed examples — all a fraction of what the equivalent full-size van brings. The segment is gone, nothing replaced it, and a good Transit Connect is the rare used vehicle with no modern substitute. Buy the straight one with the boring history and put it to work.
