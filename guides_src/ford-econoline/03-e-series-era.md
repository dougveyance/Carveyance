The 1992 redesign wrapped the proven frame in a modern body — flush glass, standard driver airbag, a Truck of the Year trophy — and then Ford simply let it run. Econoline became E-Series in 1999; the 4.9 six and Windsor V8s gave way to the Triton 4.6, 5.4 and 6.8 V10; the 7.3 Power Stroke (1995–2003) became the diesel that fleets still hoard, followed by the far more troublesome 6.0; and the 12- and 15-passenger wagons moved every church group, road crew and airport shuttle in the country. Conversion-van companies turned thousands into high-top living rooms. Through all of it the E-Series stayed America's best-selling full-size van, a streak unbroken from 1980 to the last van in June 2014.

For today's used buyer the era sorts by engine. The 4.6 is adequate and thrifty-ish at 15 EPA combined in an E-150 wagon; the 5.4 is the right answer for most; the V10 is the heavy-upfit and mountain-grade choice with 305 hp and 420 lb-ft and a thirst to match; the 7.3 diesel is priced like the legend it is; and the 6.0 diesel demands documentation of the known fixes or a hard pass. Early 5.4s and V10s also want the spark-plug-ejection history checked — the threads are famous for a reason.

## Buying one now

Fleet provenance is gold: a dealer-serviced E-250 with records beats a cheaper mystery van every time. Check frame and rockers for rust, ball joints and brake lines on anything northern, transmission service on the 4R70W/4R75 and TorqShift, and every recall closed out. Wagons need the seats and belts all present and functional, and 15-passenger examples deserve respect — load them evenly, keep the tires fresh and correctly inflated, and leave the roof rack empty; their rollover history is why the government wrote guidance about them.

Prices are the payoff. Solid working vans run $6,000–$15,000, the nicest late E-350s and diesels in the $20,000s, against $40,600 in today's money for a base 2014. Nothing else moves a crew, a camp group or 4,000 lb of tools this cheaply — which is exactly why clean ones vanish fast.
