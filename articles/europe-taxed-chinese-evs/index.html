Europe Taxed Chinese EVs. China Answered With Plug-In Hybrids.

Europe tried to slow China's electric-car expansion with tariffs. Chinese automakers changed powertrains instead, and started a new trade fight.

By Camille Bouchard · 6 minute read

When the European Union put extra tariffs on Chinese-built electric cars in 2024, the aim was simple: slow a wave of cheap, capable EVs that Volkswagen, Stellantis and Renault were struggling to match on price. China's automakers didn't retreat. They changed the product.

BYD, Chery and SAIC, which owns MG, leaned into hybrids and plug-in hybrids. Those weren't covered by the EV duties and paid only Europe's standard 10% tariff on imported cars. Instead of going through the wall, they drove around it, in cars that still offered electric driving but carried a gasoline engine as insurance. It worked. Chinese customs data show hybrid and plug-in hybrid exports to the EU were worth $7.5 billion in the first half of 2026, about 3 times the $2.5 billion of a year earlier and about 9 times the $845 million shipped in all of 2021, Reuters reported. SAIC, BYD and Chery account for more than 90% of that volume, according to the investment bank Jefferies, and hybrids are now more than half of their European sales.

Now Europe has built a second wall. Under an understanding reached with China on October 9, imports of Chinese-built hybrids and plug-in hybrids will be capped at 350,000 a year, most likely from early December, with the allowance growing 6% a year over a 4-year arrangement. A Commission official said Chinese hybrid exports to the EU were on course for as many as 800,000 this year, so the cap cuts that by more than half. The legal details are still being written, but the direction is clear: Europe no longer treats imported hybrids as a separate matter from its larger trade question with China.

Why the plug-in hybrid became China's answer

A plug-in hybrid sits in a convenient middle. It has enough electric range for many everyday trips, especially in European cities, without asking the driver to depend on public chargers, and when the battery runs down or the trip runs long, the engine keeps it moving. That makes it an easy step for a buyer who likes the idea of electric driving but isn't ready to give up gasoline. It also made the plug-in hybrid the obvious product for Chinese automakers once Europe's EV duties arrived.

An engine, electric motors, a battery and a fuel system in one car should cost more to build than an EV of similar size. China's huge battery industry and tightly integrated supply chains changed that math, letting some brands build a sophisticated plug-in hybrid and still undercut European rivals. Schmidt Automotive Research estimates that Chinese brands now sell more than a quarter of the plug-in hybrids in Europe, up from 1.7% at the start of 2024. The result wasn't just more Chinese cars in Europe. It was a different kind of Chinese car, designed and shipped because of where a tariff line fell. Trade policy had started choosing the powertrain.

The quota may reduce Chinese imports without reducing Chinese competition. It may simply move the factories.

Europe's balancing act

Brussels' stated concern is the industry at home. Car manufacturing employs millions of Europeans, and automakers there face high energy, labor and regulatory costs; the EU's 2024 investigation concluded that Chinese EV makers benefited from state subsidies, which is the basis for the EV duties. The trade-off is that limiting competition can also limit the cheaper cars that are helping electrified sales grow. If Chinese brands respond to the quota by sending their most expensive, most profitable models first, Europe could end up with fewer inexpensive hybrids and just as many premium ones.

The cap could also speed up something Europe may prefer anyway: Chinese cars built in Europe. Cars made inside the EU don't count against the quota, according to Automotive World, BYD has been building a car plant in Hungary, and analysts quoted by Reuters expect more local manufacturing. Renault's chief executive, François Provost, told Reuters the surge in Chinese hybrid imports wasn't sustainable for Europe but that a deal was better than a trade war. Felipe Munoz of Car Industry Analysis told Reuters the curbs would make life harder for Chinese automakers without stopping them, as happened with electric cars.

The question under the trade fight

Plug-in hybrids carry a question the tariff argument skips over. A plug-in that's charged regularly and used mostly for short trips can be remarkably efficient. The same car, rarely plugged in, is a heavy gasoline car hauling a battery it isn't using. Official test figures assume frequent charging, and real-world fuel use can be much higher when owners treat the cable as optional. That's true whatever the badge on the hood, Chinese or European, and it deserves as much attention as the country the car was built in.

What happens next

Chinese automakers now have choices. They can share out the quota among brands, raise prices, send their higher-margin models, build more cars in Europe or ship them to other markets. European automakers gain time, but not immunity: a quota doesn't fix slow development cycles, high costs or a shortage of genuinely cheap cars. The larger lesson is that engineering alone won't decide what replaces gasoline. Tariffs, emissions rules and local-content requirements increasingly decide what automakers build, and where.

Europe taxed Chinese EVs. China answered with plug-in hybrids, and Europe is now limiting those too. The next answer may arrive with a "Made in Europe" label on it.

The short version

Europe's 2024 tariffs on Chinese EVs pushed Chinese automakers toward hybrids and plug-in hybrids, which paid only a 10% duty, and those exports to the EU tripled in a year to $7.5 billion in the first half of 2026. Europe and China have now agreed to cap them at 350,000 a year, less than half this year's pace. The cap doesn't cover cars built in Europe, which is where the next round is likely to be fought.
