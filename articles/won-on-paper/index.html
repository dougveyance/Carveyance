The Nevera Won on Paper and Lost With Buyers

Rimac will stop the Nevera at 71 cars, well short of the 190 it planned. On paper it was among the most capable cars of its era, and that turned out not to be what the richest buyers wanted. What the hybrid hypercars that did sell got right, and what the Nevera bought anyway.

By Elliot Vance · 8 minute read

Every number that made the Rimac Nevera famous was measured in fractions of a second. It ran from a standstill to 249 mph (400 km/h) and back to a stop in 29.93 seconds, set what was then an electric production-car record on the Nürburgring Nordschleife and, in Nevera R form, reached 268 mph (431.45 km/h). The number that will follow it into the history books is a plainer one: 71.

That's how many Rimac will build, 51 Neveras and 20 Nevera Rs, against the 190 it planned. Mate Rimac gave the figures in a video on his Instagram, reported by Auto Motor und Sport and picked up widely on November 11, although the Dutch site Autoblog.nl had the 71-car total in early October. On paper, the Nevera was among the most capable cars of its era. It will finish at 37% of its plan.

What it could do

Rimac revealed the production Nevera in June 2021 at €2 million (about $2.4 million at the time, or about $3 million in today's money), with 1,914 hp and a run of 150 cars. In May 2023, at Germany's ATP Papenburg test track, it set 23 verified records in a single day, and that August it lapped the Nordschleife in 7:05.298. The Nevera R followed in 2024 at €2.3 million (about $2.5 million then, or about $2.7 million today), with 2,107 hp and a run of 40, and in 2025 it set 24 more records, cutting the 0–400–0 km/h time to 25.79 seconds. "Breaking records is in our DNA, and we won't stop here," Mate Rimac said.

Where the buyers went

Mate Rimac saw it coming. At the Financial Times' Future of the Car conference in London in May 2024, he said, "We started to develop [the] Nevera in 2016/2017, when electric was cool." Since then, he argued, the story around electric cars had changed, and "as electrification is becoming mainstream," he said, "people at the top end of the sector want to differentiate themselves." Then came the analogy: "An Apple Watch can do everything better. It can do 1000 more things, it's a lot more precise," and "it can measure your heart rate. But nobody would pay $200,000 for an Apple Watch."

"It's not about being electric; it's about doing things that other cars can't do and giving a unique experience," he said. "We do have a market for the Nevera, and it is the best-sold electric hypercar." The trouble was that by then, instant, silent thrust could be had in far cheaper electric cars. That month, he also said the company had delivered more than 50 of the 150 Neveras. The final count is 51, which suggests very few followed, though Rimac hasn't said so.

The Nevera answered every question about acceleration. Its buyers were asking a different one.

The hypercars that did sell

Mate Rimac's other car company makes the case. Bugatti Rimac's Tourbillon pairs an 8.3-liter naturally aspirated V16 with 3 electric motors for 1,800 hp. It costs €3.8 million plus taxes (more than $4 million at its 2024 launch, AFP reported, or about $4.3 million in today's money) and is limited to 250 cars. Mate Rimac told a conference held by the magazine Automobilwoche that all 250 had buyers, Motor1 reported on December 3, 2025. Back in 2024, he had said that an electric Bugatti would have sold "an amount of them, for sure, because of the brand," but, in Autocar's paraphrase, nowhere near as many as the V16 car.

The pattern holds wherever it can be checked. All 275 Mercedes-AMG Ones, with a V6 derived from Formula 1, were spoken for before production, per reports in 2017 and 2022. Koenigsegg raised its CC850 run from 50 to 70 in 2022, citing demand, and when it offered the Gemera with a 3-cylinder hybrid or a V8 hybrid, buyers chose the V8 and the 3-cylinder was dropped. Lotus's own electric Evija, by contrast, began customer deliveries in late 2024 after about a 4-year delay, and Lotus hasn't published how many it has built.

Lotus's chief executive, Feng Qingfeng, put it most plainly this year. "Certain customers simply enjoy the thrill of driving a car with a powerful engine, even with some lag in the power delivery," he told Automotive News Europe in May, as reported by The Autopian. "They somehow just don't like the smoothness of EVs." It took him a while: "Before I raced on a track, I found it difficult to understand."

Lotus goes back to a V8

Lotus's answer arrived on November 10. Horse Powertrain, the joint venture of Renault and Lotus's parent, Geely, revealed the W40: a 4.0-liter twin-turbo V8 with its turbochargers inside the V, more than 500 kW (about 670 hp), 800 Nm (590 lb-ft) and a weight of 200 kg (441 lb), designed as a hybrid from the start. Paired with a new 8-speed hybrid dual-clutch transmission, it debuts in Lotus's Type 135, due in 2028 with more than 1,000 hp combined. Lotus hasn't confirmed the name, though Auto Express reports that it's known internally as the new Esprit.

It isn't Lotus's first V8. The Esprit V8, with Lotus's own 3.5-liter twin-turbo engine, ran until 2004. "When the Esprit went out of production [in 2004], it had a V8 engine," Lotus's design chief, Ben Payne, told Autocar, "and it's something people have asked to come back ever since." The company that built the Evija is betting its next flagship on fuel, with a battery to help.

What a used Nevera is worth

There's just 1 public sale price to go on, and it needs care. A 2024 Nevera, chassis 69 of 150, with 3,788 miles, sold on duPont Registry Live for $1,352,400 including a 5% buyer's premium, according to a duPont Registry roundup dated November 8, which gives no sale date. By our calculation, that's about 56% of the Nevera's launch price in 2021 dollars, or about 45% in today's money. But the list price was in euros, this car's options and taxes aren't known, and 1 sale doesn't make a market.

What the Nevera bought

Mate Rimac isn't apologizing. "I think it is still a pretty good achievement. It's the most sold electric hypercar in the world," he said in his video. "And without that, there wouldn't be the Tourbillon today; there wouldn't be this factory." His verdict: "So, it did its job." And Nurdin Pitarevic, then Rimac Technology's chief operating officer and its chief executive since February, told Autocar last December that there's unlikely to be another Rimac car after the Nevera.

So the Nevera won the argument about how hard an electric car can accelerate and lost the one about what a hypercar is for. At this price, buyers pay for a sound, a story and something nobody else can have, and by the time the Nevera reached them, electric speed was the thing everybody could have. "I think it's a quite cool car...that will have its place in history," Rimac said. Both halves of that are right.

The short version

Rimac will end the Nevera at 71 cars against 190 planned, despite a string of acceleration records and a 268 mph top speed that BYD's Yangwang U9 passed within weeks. Mate Rimac's own explanation, from 2024, was that electric performance stopped being special once it went mainstream. The hypercars that sold, such as Bugatti's V16 Tourbillon, kept combustion engines, with hybrid help, and Lotus is following with a hybrid V8 for 2028. Rimac says the Nevera still made the Tourbillon and its factory possible.
