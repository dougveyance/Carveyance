The second GT exists because Ford wanted to win Le Mans again on the 50th anniversary of 1966 — and on June 19, 2016, the #68 Ford Chip Ganassi car did exactly that, taking the GTE-Pro class before the road car reached a single customer. What Multimatic then built in Markham, Ontario was the race car with license plates: carbon monocoque, pushrod suspension, active aero, and the 3.5 twin-turbo EcoBoost V6 at 647 hp — 660 from 2020 — driving through a 7-speed dual-clutch. Car and Driver measured 3.0 to 60, a 10.8-second quarter and 1.11 g; Ford claimed 216 mph. Nothing else at the price was this uncompromising, including the seats, which don't move (the pedals do).

Buying one new required an application: Ford vetted collectors, racers and influencers, bound them to no-flip agreements, and allocated 1,350 cars across 6 model years salted with specials — the Heritage liveries, the $750,000 exposed-weave Liquid Carbon, and the track-only farewell pair of 45 Mk IIs and 67 Mk IVs at up to $1.7 million.

## Buying one now

Every car is documented from birth, so this market runs on spec and story: Heritage editions and Liquid Carbon lead, launch-year 2017s carry first-of-breed cachet, and the handful of cars with real miles trade honestly against trailer queens. Verify the allocation history and any contract-period transfer, confirm software and service are current at a certified GT dealer (annual fluid and inspection schedules matter on a dry-sump race engine), and treat paint-protection film as the norm rather than a flag. The V6 question answers itself from the driver's seat — the induction noise is pure prototype racer, and nobody who has heard it at full boost asks about cylinders again.

Values sit comfortably above the original stickers and the specials multiply from there, which makes the standard-spec driver's car the quiet value: same monocoque, same Le Mans blood, same 1-of-1,350 math.
