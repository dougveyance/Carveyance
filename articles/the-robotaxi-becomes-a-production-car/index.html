The Robotaxi Is Becoming a Production Car

Every Waymo so far has been a car someone else finished building, then fitted with sensors. Toyota's plan to do the whole job in the plant it opened for the first Corolla is how a self-driving science project starts to look like an ordinary product.

By Oliver Bacon · 7 minute read

In September 1966, Toyota opened a plant in Toyota City for a single car. The Corolla needed a factory that could build 20,000 cars a month, and Takaoka was it. 60 years later, a week before the Corolla's 60th birthday, Toyota said Takaoka would build something the Corolla's engineers couldn't have imagined: a car that drives itself, for Waymo.

The announcement on October 30 was short on numbers. Toyota will build a custom version of its bZ4X electric SUV, with more rear legroom, automatic doors, a power liftgate and ambient lighting. "The entire process will take place under one roof, from assembling the base vehicle to integrating Waymo's AV hardware," the companies said. Testing begins in Japan in 2027, followed by the UK and other Waymo markets in later years. Neither company said how many cars, at what cost or in which cities. Hiroki Nakajima, Toyota's executive vice president, said they would start with a small number, Reuters reported. But the shape of the deal matters more than its size, because it changes how a robotaxi gets made.

How Waymo builds a car today

Waymo has always been a software company that needed someone else's cars. Its fleet is mostly Jaguar I-Paces, built by Magna Steyr in Austria until Jaguar ended production in 2024, then shipped to a plant Waymo runs with Magna in Mesa, Arizona, where the lidars, radars, cameras and computers go on. Its newest model, the Ojai, arrives from Zeekr in China as an incomplete "rolling glider" and is finished in Mesa. A version of Hyundai's Ioniq 5, built in Georgia, is still in testing.

That approach has worked well enough to get Waymo to more than 500,000 rides a week, a figure Alphabet gave in April, and in October Sundar Pichai, Alphabet's chief executive, said Waymo operates in 15 American cities and has driven more than 300 million miles with nobody at the wheel. But it's a conversion business. A finished car is partly taken apart and rebuilt around hardware it wasn't designed for, and every step adds cost, time and another set of hands. When Mesa opened in May 2025, Waymo's head of vehicle production, Kent Yiu, said it would "certainly need to be capable of doing tens of thousands per year." Toyota builds that many cars in a few days.

Why Toyota, and why Japan first

The first answer is the steering wheel's side of the car. "We'll start with the right-hand drive markets in Japan and the UK," Dmitri Dolgov, Waymo's co-chief executive, said, according to Reuters. Waymo's American fleet is no use on the left side of the road, and Toyota builds right-hand-drive cars in volume. Waymo already plans a driverless taxi service in Tokyo in 2027 with the taxi company Nihon Kotsu and the GO app, starting with I-Paces and growing to about 100 cars, subject to approval.

The second is the market. Japan had about 220,000 taxi drivers in March 2023, roughly 20% fewer than 4 years earlier, according to the transport ministry, and a record 82 taxi companies went bankrupt or closed in 2024, with driver shortages behind more than 40% of the bankruptcies, according to the research firm Teikoku Databank. A robotaxi that a Japanese company builds in Japan is an easier conversation with regulators and riders than an imported one. Toyota's pitch was safety. "Safety technology makes the biggest difference when it reaches as many people as possible," Nakajima said. "No single company can achieve the goal of zero traffic accidents alone, but with partners like Waymo, we will get there one step at a time."

A robotaxi stops being a science project when it comes off the same line as everything else.

The opposite of the Cybercab

Tesla is making the other bet. It designs the Cybercab itself, a 2-seater with no steering wheel or pedals, and builds it in its own plant in Texas. That gives Tesla control and a car designed from the start for no driver. It also gives Tesla a regulator's audit, because federal safety standards still assume a driver, and its sworn answers to NHTSA's 21 questions about how it certified the car were due on October 30. Waymo's cars, so far, have been ordinary cars that already meet the rules, steering wheel and all, which is less dramatic and much easier to regulate.

Waymo's lead has been expensive. Alphabet's Other Bets, which is mostly Waymo, lost $2.05 billion in the third quarter, and Waymo raised $16 billion in February at a $126 billion valuation. A partner that builds at scale doesn't fix that on its own, but it's likely a cheaper way to add cars than converting them one at a time.

The part nobody promised

The sentence that will get the most attention is the vaguest. Toyota and Waymo "will continue to explore the future of autonomous driving technology for personally owned Toyota vehicles," the companies said, the same idea they first raised in April 2025. That's not a plan to sell a self-driving Toyota, and Dolgov said Waymo had no other countries, timelines or specifics to share. But it's the possibility that makes this more than a supply deal. If the world's biggest carmaker can build a Waymo on its own line, the line between a robotaxi and a car you own gets thinner.

For now, the real news is smaller and more practical. The robotaxi is moving out of the conversion shop and onto an assembly line, at the plant that once taught Toyota how to build the world's most ordinary car. That's the right place for it. The point of a self-driving car was always to make getting around boring.

The short version

Toyota will build a custom bZ4X for Waymo at Takaoka, the plant it opened in 1966 for the first Corolla, with Waymo's hardware fitted on the same line. Testing starts in Japan in 2027, then the UK. Today every Waymo is converted after another automaker builds it. No numbers, costs or cities were given, and self-driving Toyotas for private owners are only being "explored."
