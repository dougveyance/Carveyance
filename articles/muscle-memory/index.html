Muscle Memory

Ram's V8 Rumble Bee sold out its first year's allocation in 90 minutes. The muscle truck has been here before: a short history of the hot-rod pickup, from the Li'l Red Express to a 777-hp Hemi.

By Diana Chambers · 6 minute read

Stellantis tucked the line into its third-quarter sales release on October 1: "Orders opened for 2027 Ram 1500 Rumble Bee 5.7L and initial allocation for the 2026 calendar year sold out in 90 minutes."

A short-cab, wide-body pickup with a 395-hp Hemi V8 and a price above $60,000 sold out in less time than it takes to watch a movie. The muscle truck is back, and it has been back before.

What Ram built

Ram revealed the Rumble Bee on May 20 and called it the start of a new "pickup performance subsegment." Every version is a Quad Cab with a short bed and widened fenders, on a wheelbase Ram has shortened by 13 inches. Every one has full-time four-wheel drive through a BorgWarner transfer case, with a button that disconnects the front axle for rear-wheel drive only. All use an 8-speed automatic.

The Track Pack adds air suspension and 325/40R22 tires. The SRT is quick enough to run a quarter mile in 11.6 seconds at 116 mph, by Ram's figures, and Ram is targeting a 170 mph top speed. Mopar Insiders reports all 4 are built at Stellantis's Saltillo truck plant in Mexico.

Read the fine print on "sold out"

What sold out was the "initial allocation for the 2026 calendar year," the trucks Ram planned to deliver this year, not a full year of production. Ram hasn't said how many trucks that was. Orders opened on August 13, and by late August Autoevolution was already reporting dealers who had used up their allocations.

It is still a strong signal. Ram's sales rose 29% in the quarter, and Ram 1500 sales jumped 73%, to 76,650.

The Hemi came back first

The Rumble Bee follows a reversal. For 2025 Ram dropped the 5.7-liter Hemi V8 from the Ram 1500 and replaced it with the 3.0-liter Hurricane inline-6. In June 2025 it announced the Hemi's return for 2026. "Ram screwed up when we dropped the Hemi," Ram chief Tim Kuniskis said at the time, according to Motor1. "We own it and we fixed it." AutoGuide reported more than 10,000 orders in the first 24 hours.

There's a market reason a V8 is easier to sell now than it was 2 years ago. Since July 2025, the federal fine for missing a fuel-economy target has been $0, so a V8 truck no longer costs its maker penalties or credits to cover. The $1,300 Question explains how that rule works.

The muscle truck has been here before

The name isn't new, and neither is the idea. American automakers have built hot-rod pickups whenever there was room for a little fun, usually from parts already on the shelf.

What's different this time

The old muscle trucks were mostly parts-bin speed: a big engine from elsewhere in the lineup, a short bed, a lowered stance and a stripe. At the bottom of the range, the price has climbed less than you might think. The 1999 Lightning cost about $58,800 in today's money; the cheapest Rumble Bee is $62,790. At the top, the SRT is closer to $100,000.

The engineering has moved on too. Most of the old ones drove the rear wheels; the Syclone, with all-wheel drive, was the exception. Every Rumble Bee has full-time four-wheel drive, with rear-wheel drive at the push of a button, and its fastest version is more than 3 times as powerful as the Li'l Red Express.

What hasn't changed is the appeal. The muscle truck has never been about what a pickup is for. It's about a full-size truck that goes faster than it looks like it should, and in 90 minutes this fall, enough people wanted one to empty the first year's order book.

The short version

Ram's 2027 Rumble Bee, a short-cab, wide-body V8 pickup from $62,790, sold out its 2026 allocation in 90 minutes, on the back of the Hemi's return and a 73% jump in Ram 1500 sales. It's the latest in a line that runs from the 1978 Li'l Red Express through the Syclone, Lightning and SRT-10, now with full-time four-wheel drive and up to 777 hp.
