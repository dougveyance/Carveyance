Supply explains everything here. 4,038 first-generation cars and 1,350 second — the totals are fixed, the audience keeps growing, and both generations skipped the depreciation phase entirely. That rewrites the usual used-exotic playbook: there is no "wait for the dip," no high-mile bargain tier that threatens the market, and no mystery inventory. What moves individual prices is spec and story — Heritage liveries and low production salt at the top, standard colors and healthy mileage at the accessible end — and the spread between a driven car and a sealed one is the main decision a buyer makes.

The 2 generations attract different money. The 2005 is the analog classic: V8, manual, usable, increasingly a show-field fixture, with the 346 Gulf Heritage cars as its headline lot. The 2017 is the modern-collectible play: application provenance, Le Mans anniversary story, and specials — Liquid Carbon, the Heritage series, the 112 track-only Mk IIs and Mk IVs — that trade in 7 figures. Both sail through auction season every year, which means pricing is unusually transparent for a car this rare; the comps are always fresh.

## How to buy well

Buy the documentation as much as the car: window sticker and option verification on a 2005, allocation file and service record on a 2017. Favor the spec the market already loves unless you're buying to drive — then favor the car with miles, because its owner maintained it as a machine rather than a certificate. Have a marque specialist inspect either generation (clamshell fit and fuel-system service on the first; software currency and underbody carbon on the second), and remember that insurance, storage and annual service are real numbers at this altitude.

And keep the perspective that makes the GT special: this is the only American car line where the entry ticket is a Ford badge and the company is Ferrari, Porsche and McLaren money. The legend was earned at Le Mans twice, 50 years apart. The market has simply never stopped paying attention.
