Gas In, Electric Out

The 2027 Nissan Rogue Hybrid drives every mile on electric motors and never needs a plug, because its gasoline engine is only there to make electricity. How e-Power works, where it beats the hybrids Americans already know, and where it doesn't.

By Oliver Bacon · 8 minute read

For 25 years, a hybrid in America has meant a gasoline engine and an electric motor sharing the job of turning the wheels. Sometimes one pushes, sometimes the other, sometimes both. Nissan's new Rogue Hybrid breaks that arrangement. Its engine never touches the wheels at all.

On September 23 Nissan detailed the 2027 Rogue Hybrid, the first car in America with its e-Power system. 2 electric motors do all the driving, which makes all-wheel drive standard. A turbocharged 1.5-liter 3-cylinder runs a generator to keep them supplied. There is no charging port, because there is nothing to charge it from but the gas tank.

Nissan calls it the only hybrid in its class with a 100% electric drivetrain. Here is how it works, what that buys you, and the one place it doesn't help much.

How it works

Think of it as an electric car that carries its own small power station. The engine burns gasoline and spins a generator. The electricity goes through an inverter to a small battery and to the 2 motors: 202 hp at the front axle and 134 hp at the rear, 225 hp working together. There is no gearbox connecting the engine to anything but the generator.

Because the engine isn't tied to road speed, it can run at whatever speed and load it burns fuel most efficiently, and switch off when the battery is full. Nissan says its third-generation engine turns 42% of the fuel's energy into work, a very high figure for a gasoline engine. When you slow down, the motors run backward as generators and put energy back into the battery.

From behind the wheel, it should feel like an electric car. The front motor makes its full 243 lb-ft from a standstill, and there are no shifts. Nissan's e-Pedal lets the driver slow the car by lifting off the accelerator, and its Smooth Stop feature eases off the brakes at the end of a stop so heads don't nod forward.

3 ways to build a hybrid

Most hybrids Americans have driven work differently, and the difference explains everything about where e-Power shines.

Honda's system is the closest relative. At low and moderate speeds a CR-V Hybrid also drives on its motor, with the engine making electricity when it's needed. But when it settles into a steady cruise, a clutch locks the engine to the wheels, because at that point driving them directly is the more efficient path. Nissan's e-Power has no such clutch.

The range extender is a cousin with a plug. Ram's range-extended pickup and the gasoline range-extended F-150 that Ford plans for 2027 use the same idea with a much bigger battery that also charges from the wall. Why the gains come in town

Every conversion costs a little energy. Turning the engine's motion into electricity, then turning that electricity back into motion at the wheels, loses a few percent at each step. In stop-and-go driving that cost is easily repaid: the engine runs only at its best, often not at all, and braking energy goes back into the battery. At a steady 70 mph there is little braking to recover and the engine is running anyway, so the detour through the generator gains little. That's the job Honda's clutch exists for.

The clearest way to see it is to set the hybrid's estimates beside the gasoline Rogue's official ratings.

In the city the hybrid's estimate is 43% better. On the highway it's 1 mpg better. Nissan has been working on exactly that: it says the third-generation e-Power in this Rogue improves highway fuel consumption by 14% over the version before it, and real-world fuel economy by 16%.

Where it came from

e-Power isn't new, just new here. Nissan launched it in Japan in November 2016 in the Note, a small hatchback, and that month the Note became Japan's best-selling car. Europe got it in the Qashqai crossover. Nissan says nearly 2 million e-Power vehicles have been sold in 68 countries since 2016.

The version in the Rogue is the third generation, launched in Europe in 2025. It packs the motor, generator, inverter and 2 gear sets into a single 5-in-1 unit that is smaller and lighter, and Nissan says it is up to 5.6 decibels quieter under load. The Rogue Hybrid is built at Nissan's Kyushu plant in Japan.

It isn't Nissan's only electrified Rogue. A Rogue Plug-In Hybrid went on sale for 2026, Nissan's first plug-in hybrid in the U.S., built around Mitsubishi's Outlander system. e-Power is Nissan's own design.

Against the hybrids Americans know

The Rogue Hybrid goes straight at the best-selling compact SUVs, and on fuel economy it lands in the middle of the group, not at the top.

The RAV4 still has the best fuel economy in every column. Nissan's case is the drive: a car that feels electric all the time, all-wheel drive on every trim, and an e-Pedal that Nissan says no rival in the class offers. Whether buyers pay for that feel is the open question.

Is it worth it over the gasoline Rogue?

The 2027 Rogue Hybrid SV starts at $35,490 before destination. The 2026 gasoline Rogue SV with all-wheel drive started at $31,890, a gap of $3,600. The 2 model years aren't equipped identically, so treat that as the size of the gap, not an exact price for the hybrid parts.

That table is the whole pitch in 3 lines. For a commuter in traffic, e-Power is a real saving and a nicer way to drive. For someone who mostly cruises the interstate, it's a nicer way to drive, and that's about all.

Why Nissan is doing it now

Hybrids reached a record 16% of new-vehicle sales in the second quarter of this year, according to the Energy Information Administration, and Toyota now sells the RAV4 only as a hybrid or plug-in hybrid. Until now Nissan had no conventional hybrid to offer in its best-selling SUV, only the gasoline Rogue and a plug-in built on someone else's system. e-Power gives it something no rival has, which matters more to a carmaker coming from behind than winning the mpg column.

The first cars, SRs with the Technology Package at $43,065, arrive this fall. The rest of the lineup, from $37,065, follows in early 2027.

The short version

e-Power is an electric drivetrain with a gasoline generator on board: no plug, no gearbox, all-wheel drive standard. It's at its best in stop-and-go traffic, where Nissan estimates 40 mpg against the gasoline Rogue's 28. On the highway it barely beats the gas version, and the RAV4 Hybrid still beats it everywhere on paper.
