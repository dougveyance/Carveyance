The Civic is an easy car to buy in a hurry: there are plenty of them, they hold their value and the badge reassures people. That's the reason to slow down. A clean, complete service record matters more here than a shiny paint job, and on an Si or a Type R, so does an honest account of how the car has been driven.

## First, know which one it is

Decode the VIN to confirm the year, body, engine and transmission, and check the window sticker if the seller has it. The traps: the coupe ended after 2020; there was no Si for 2021 and no Type R for 2022; the turbo 1.5-liter left the regular Civic after 2024; and a "Sport Touring" is a turbo hatchback for 2022–2024 but a hybrid from 2025. A listing whose year, trim, gearbox and horsepower don't agree isn't necessarily dishonest, but it does need checking.

## The test drive

Start the car from cold and listen for rough running and rattles. On a CVT, accelerate gently and then hard; it should feel smooth, without shudders. On a manual, check where the clutch bites, make sure it doesn't slip in a high gear, and shift quickly between second and third to check the synchros. On a 2022–2025 car, turn the wheel slowly from lock to lock in a parking lot and note any heavy or sticky spots, a symptom of the steering gearbox recall. Run the air conditioning on full cold, pair your phone and check every camera and driver-assistance warning.

## Engine checks

On a 2016–2018 Civic with the 1.5-liter turbo, check the oil level and smell the dipstick for fuel, especially on a car from a cold state that has done mostly short trips. Honda extended the warranty on the camshafts, rocker arms and spark plugs to 6 years with unlimited miles, but most of those cars are now outside it. On the 2.0-liter, the main things are regular oil changes and a smooth idle. On an Si or Type R, ask what has been modified and look for aftermarket intakes, turbo plumbing and tuning software.

## The A/C warranties

Honda extended 2 air conditioning warranties to 10 years from the original sale, with unlimited miles: the condenser on 2016–2020 sedans and coupes and 2017–2021 hatchbacks and Type Rs, and the compressor shaft seal on 2016–2021 Civics, including the Type R. The condenser extension covers only condensers that were defective from the factory, not damage, and the seal extension doesn't apply to a car with a salvage or branded title. If the air blows warm, ask a Honda dealer to check the VIN before you agree to a price.

## Recalls: check by VIN

- **2016–2021:** 23V858. The fuel pump in the tank may fail. Other fuel pump recalls cover 2018–2019 hatchbacks and Type Rs (20V314) and 2019–2020 Civics (21V215).
- **2017:** 17V706. The right halfshaft on some sedans and coupes was improperly heat treated and may break.
- **2017–2018:** 18V663. A magnet in the electric power steering's torque sensor can come loose. 18V266 (2017 hatchback and Type R): replacement seatback pads without air bag openings.
- **2020–2021:** 23V458. A fastener between the brake booster and master cylinder may be improperly assembled.
- **2016–2022:** 26V332, and 24V064 for 2020–2022. The front passenger seat weight sensor can crack, so the passenger air bag may not work correctly.
- **2022–2025, including the 2023–2025 Type R and 2025 Hybrid:** 24V744. Friction inside the steering gearbox can make steering difficult. 23V704: 2022–2024 cars that got a replacement steering rack in service.
- **2023:** 23V430. The stability control unit can leak brake fluid. 24V859 (2023–2024): a driver's seat cushion frame that may not be tightened.
- **2025 Civic and Civic Hybrid:** 24V763. The high-pressure fuel pump may crack and leak fuel.
- **Every year:** run the VIN at [NHTSA's recall lookup](https://www.nhtsa.gov/recalls) and ask for proof that each repair was done.

## Walk away if

The car has a branded title, flood damage or repaired structural damage. Walk away from an Si or Type R whose seller won't say what has been changed, from tuning software with no way back to stock, and from any car whose seller won't allow an independent inspection.

## What is it worth?

We don't publish average prices. They go stale quickly, and an average that mixes a 2017 LX sedan with a 2023 Type R tells you nothing. Compare 20 to 30 listings for the same generation, body, engine, transmission, trim and mileage, with the same title history, and keep dealer asking prices separate from what cars actually sold for. [FitDrive's deal check](/fitdrive/) does the first part for you. Cross-shop a Toyota Corolla, Mazda3 or Hyundai Elantra of the same age, and take the cost of tires, brakes and overdue service off the price. On an Si or Type R, an unmodified car with records is worth more than one with expensive parts, not less.

Buying from a private seller, or keeping the Civic past its factory warranty? Service plan providers to compare include [Chaiz](https://www.chaiz.com), [Endurance](https://www.endurancewarranty.com) and [Olive](https://www.olive.com). Carveyance doesn't rank or recommend them. Read what each one covers and excludes, especially for turbochargers, the hybrid system and modified cars, and see the [FTC's guidance on auto service contracts](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2023/03/what-know-about-auto-service-contracts-extended-warranty-scams) first.

## Our shortlist

- **Best all-rounder:** a 2025 or later Civic Hybrid with its recalls done.
- **Best value:** a 2019–2021 sedan with the 2.0-liter engine and records.
- **Most practical:** a 2022–2024 EX-L or Sport Touring hatchback with the steering recall done.
- **Best to drive on a budget:** a stock 2017–2020 or 2022 and later Si.
- **The performance car:** a stock 2020–2021 Type R, or a 2023 or later one.
