Camilo Pardo's design, J Mays' direction, Carroll Shelby consulting — the 2005 GT was the GT40 redrawn at a scale that fit actual humans, and the only reason it isn't called GT40 is a trademark squabble. An aluminum spaceframe carried the supercharged 5.4 with its Eaton screw blower, 550 hp and 500 lb-ft through a Ricardo 6-speed, and the numbers landed exactly where the shape promised: 3.3 seconds to 60 for Car and Driver, 3.7 in MotorWeek's hands, 205 mph verified. Against the Ferrari and Lamborghini of the day it gave up nothing but gave you a clutch pedal, sight lines, and air conditioning that worked.

At $139,995 — about $241,100 in 2026 dollars, and nudged to $149,995 mid-run — it was the bargain of the segment, and 4,038 were built through 2006 including 346 Gulf-liveried Heritage editions. The famous footnote: these cars never depreciated. Not in 2008, not ever. Early owners drove a supercar for free, which remains the best trick in modern collecting.

## Buying one now

Condition grading is mileage-and-stripes arithmetic. The 4 big options — stripes, painted calipers, forged BBS wheels, McIntosh stereo — move money, Heritage cars lead everything, and documented low-owner examples with the books and the car cover bring the premiums. But this is also the rare collectible where a 20,000-mile car is a feature for a driver-buyer: the drivetrain is robust, parts flow through Ford channels, and specialists know every bolt. Check for paint work from badly-fitted clamshell adjustments, confirm the fuel tank's service history (a known age item), and insist on the window sticker — option verification is half the value conversation.

Drive one before you garage it. The steering, the blower whine, the gearbox — this is the analog American supercar at its peak, and the cars with miles are the ones whose owners understood what they had.
