Why America's New Kei Truck Tops Out at 25 MPH

Toyota's revived Scion brand starts with the KEI-01, a mini truck to be built in Indiana and capped at 25 mph. That cap lets it skip crash tests and airbags, and it explains why kei-truck fans still import 25-year-old trucks.

By Oliver Bacon · 9 minute read

Toyota's newest truck has selectable 4-wheel drive, air conditioning, a bed that folds open on 3 sides and room for 272 kg (600 lb) of cargo. It also tops out at 25 mph (40 km/h), and that isn't an engineering shortfall. It's the ticket in. The Scion KEI-01, shown on November 9 and announced the next day as the first vehicle of a revived Scion brand, can use American public roads because federal law files it beside the neighborhood electric vehicle, not the pickup.

The truck has a 660 cc 3-cylinder, a CVT, "selectable four-wheel drive (4WD) with auto mode and push-button lock," and right-hand drive. Toyota Indiana will build it from the second half of 2027, with $34 million and about 50 new jobs, for "a fleet-focused pilot program" at selected Toyota dealers. Retail sales are something Toyota "may consider" later.

Toyota hasn't given a price or a horsepower figure, or confirmed which Japanese truck it's based on. Top Gear, The Drive and The Truth About Cars identify it as Japan's Toyota Pixis Truck or the Daihatsu Hijet it's based on, and the numbers fit. The Pixis is 3,395 mm long and 1,475 mm wide, which squares with Toyota's "just over 11 feet in length and under 5 feet in width." Toyota rates the KEI-01 for less cargo, though its 4WD, with an auto mode and a push-button lock, matches the system Daihatsu gave CVT Hijets in 2021.

What makes a low-speed vehicle

A low-speed vehicle, or LSV, is a motor vehicle "that is 4-wheeled," rated under 1,361 kg (3,000 lb) fully loaded, whose top speed over a mile is above 20 mph and "not more than 40 kilometers per hour (25 miles per hour) on a paved level surface." Its standard, FMVSS No. 500, asks only for "the minimum motor vehicle equipment appropriate for motor vehicle safety," and the list runs to 12 items: headlamps, front and rear turn signals, taillamps, stop lamps, red reflectors, mirrors, a parking brake, a safety-glass windshield, a VIN, a seat belt at every seat, rear visibility to part of FMVSS No. 111 and an alert sound. Airbags and crash tests aren't on it.

Why it gets to skip the crash tests

NHTSA wrote the rule in 1998, as golf cars and 25 mph neighborhood electric vehicles turned up on public streets in growing numbers. Citing their "limited-speed capability and relatively controlled operating environments," it decided that "standards applicable to current passenger cars could not, and need not, be applied in all aspects to LSVs," and it left road access to the states. According to the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety, NHTSA widened the class to 1,361 kg (3,000 lb) in 2006, and, IIHS noted in 2010, it required no airbags or other crash protection beyond belts.

IIHS showed the cost of that bargain in 2010. A GEM e2 low-speed vehicle hit in the side at 31 mph (50 km/h) produced dummy readings indicating severe or fatal injuries. A 2008 Changan Tiger Star minitruck at 25 mph, meeting a 2010 Ford Ranger at 35 mph, left its dummy with likely serious neck and severe leg injuries, while the Ranger's was unscathed. "There's a world of difference between vehicles that meet crashworthiness standards and those that don't," said David Zuby, then the institute's chief research officer.

Where 25 mph is allowed to go

NHTSA decides what an LSV must carry; the states decide where it may go, and most draw the line at roads posted 35 mph (56 km/h) or less. Florida, whose statute names mini trucks alongside LSVs, says they "may be operated only on streets where the posted speed limit is 35 miles per hour or less." California bars them from faster roads except to cross at an intersection that "begins and ends on a roadway with a speed limit of 35 miles per hour or less." Rhode Island uses the same line and lets towns restrict them further. Delaware is plainest: "Low-speed vehicles may only be operated on roads where the posted speed limit is not more than 35 miles per hour." Its own LSV definition excludes trucks, a wrinkle for Delaware fleets.

How Japan's kei class works

The truck underneath comes from a class Japan defines from the outside in: no more than 3.40 meters (133.9 in) long, 1.48 meters (58.3 in) wide and 2.00 meters (78.7 in) tall, with an engine of no more than 660 cc. The 64 PS (47 kW) power ceiling isn't law at all but an agreement among Japan's carmakers, pegged, Kuruma News reports, to the 1987 Suzuki Alto Works, then the most powerful kei car. Unrestricted kei imports still generally top 70 mph and cruise near 65, according to The Autopian, so the KEI-01's 25 mph is a legal ceiling, not the class's natural pace.

Why fans import 25-year-old trucks

New kei trucks can't simply be imported for road use, because each would need certifying to the full federal standards, and most are too powerful or too large to qualify as LSVs, according to the law firm Covington & Burling. The way in is age, thanks to the Imported Vehicle Safety Compliance Act of 1988. In NHTSA's words, "A motor vehicle that is at least 25 years old can be lawfully imported into the U.S." The clock "runs from the date of the vehicle's manufacture," so trucks built in November 2001 become eligible this month.

A clean 1999–2001 truck fetches $2,500 to $4,500 at auction in Japan before shipping and duty, says the car shipper W.C. Shipping, and The Autopian puts a delivered one under $5,000. "NHTSA cannot endorse their use on public highways," the agency wrote in a 2009 letter, adding that "registration and licensing are generally matters of State law." That sentence is where the trouble starts.

The fight over plates

In 2021, AAMVA, the association of state motor-vehicle administrators, recommended treating mini trucks, even those over 25 years old, as off-road vehicles that shouldn't be titled or registered for the road without US safety and emissions labels. Several states followed, and owners fought back.

Who it's for, and who it isn't

That makes the KEI-01 a tool for places where 25 mph is plenty: a ranch, a university or corporate campus, a job site. Carscoops says Toyota is aiming at farmers, ranchers, towns, campuses and small businesses. It should also sidestep the fight over plates, because it arrives built to a federal safety standard, the very thing AAMVA says the imports lack.

It isn't a tiny Tacoma, though, or a truck for a 45 mph state road. That job still belongs to an old import, which can keep up with traffic where a state lets it on faster roads, for under $5,000 delivered, but brings its own headaches. It's at least 25 years old, its plates depend on the state and sometimes the year, and, like the KEI-01, it was never built to American crash standards, only it does its driving at highway speed. If Toyota opens retail sales, it will be selling a kei truck with a clean federal pedigree and a top speed many buyers won't accept.

The short version

The KEI-01 is road-legal as a low-speed vehicle under FMVSS No. 500, a 1998 rule that now requires 12 basic items but no crash tests or airbags, provided it can't top 25 mph. States usually limit LSVs to roads posted 35 mph or less. Fans import 25-year-old kei trucks that cruise at 65, though some states fight registering them. The KEI-01 is a fleet tool, not a highway truck.
