The 2014 redesign moved production to Valencia and the chassis onto the Focus and Escape's front-drive platform, which is why period road tests keep sounding faintly astonished — this was a van with steering feel. The standard 2.5 Duratec made 169 hp and shrugged off fleet abuse; the optional 1.6 EcoBoost made 178 hp and 184 lb-ft and hit 60 in 8.7 seconds in Motor Trend's long-wheelbase cargo test. The 2019 refresh brought a new face, a 162-hp 2.0 with direct injection, and an 8-speed automatic that finally gave it relaxed highway legs. 2 wheelbases and cargo or passenger bodies covered the trades; the 7-seat Wagon — Ford's first since the 2007 Freestar — covered families, hotels and New York's accessible yellow-cab fleet.

A short-wheelbase cargo van launched at $22,000, about $31,300 in 2026 dollars, and EPA figures of 24–25 combined made the operating-cost case against full-size vans all by themselves. It worked until the segment didn't: sales peaked at 52,221 in 2015, the NV200 and Metris left the market, and Ford ended North American sales after 2023 — shelving a planned Mexican-built third generation on the way out.

## Which one

The 2019–2023 2.0 with the 8-speed is the pick for anyone covering highway miles — smoother, quieter, better equipped, and the volume year to shop is 2019's 41,598. The 1.6 EcoBoost is the enthusiast's choice and the one to inspect hardest: turbo vans want documented oil changes, and the 1.6 has a known appetite for coolant attention, so walk from any example with an unexplained low reservoir. The 2.5 is the taxi-grade constant — slower, nearly unkillable, and the engine most ex-fleet bargains carry.

Wagons deserve a special look. A 7-seat Titanium is a minivan that fits in a compact parking space, and because nobody cross-shops it against the Odyssey, it prices like the work van it resembles. Ex-taxis are the flip side: honest, roadworthy, and carrying the mileage of 3 normal vans — buy on maintenance paper or not at all.
