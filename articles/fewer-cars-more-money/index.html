At the Top of the Car Market, Fewer Cars Make More Money

Ferrari shipped fewer cars this quarter and raised its forecast. BMW sold more in America and Europe and cut its own. And an F50 that cost about $475,000 new (about $1.05 million today) just sold for $18.7 million. Where the money in cars goes now, and why so little of it reaches the middle.

By Douglas James · 8 minute read

The car business has always kept score in units. More cars built, more cars sold, more market share. In the first week of November, the 2 most famous names in European performance cars reported the same quarter and showed how little that score now explains. Ferrari shipped 33 fewer cars than a year earlier and made 13% more money. BMW grew in America and Europe and told investors its car business would earn roughly nothing this year.

The same week, at an auction in Las Vegas, a 29-year-old Ferrari with 106 miles on it sold for $18,705,000. The car market is splitting by price, and the line isn't between cheap cars and expensive ones. It runs between the very top and everyone else.

2 companies, 1 quarter

BMW Group delivered 551,075 cars from July to September, down 6.3%. Its revenue fell 5.8% to €30.45 billion (about $34.1 billion), and its net profit fell 14.7% to €1.45 billion (about $1.62 billion). The margin in its car business was 3.4%, down from 5.2%. Then it cut its forecast for the full year to an automotive EBIT margin of −0.5% to +0.5%. That was its second cut in less than 5 months, from 4% to 6% at the start of the year.

Ferrari shipped 3,368 cars, down 1%. Its revenue rose 7% to €1.90 billion (about $2.12 billion), its net profit rose 13% to €431 million (about $483 million), and its EBIT margin rose to 29.7%. It raised its forecast for the second time this year. "Our order book now reaches into 2028," said Benedetto Vigna, its chief executive.

Both companies lost ground in China, and Ferrari lost more of it in percentage terms: its shipments to mainland China, Hong Kong and Taiwan fell from 248 to 143, which Vigna put down to a model changeover. BMW's fell from about 152,000 to 114,355. The difference is what each company does with the cars it does sell.

Why selling fewer works for Ferrari

Ferrari builds fewer cars than people want, on purpose, and charges for everything else. Personalization, the paint, leather, carbon fiber and one-off details buyers add to their cars, passed 21% of Ferrari's revenue from cars and spare parts this quarter, its finance chief, Antonio Picca Piccon, said on the earnings call. The year started with a plan of about 20%. The mix keeps getting richer, too: the quarter was carried by the €3.6 million (about $4 million) F80, the 849 Testarossa and the 12Cilindri, while older models such as the Roma Spider and the 296 GTS were phased out.

That's why a 42% drop in China barely shows up in the results. When the order book runs into 2028, a buyer who walks away is replaced by one who has been waiting. The Luce, Ferrari's first electric car, reaches its first customers this quarter, and Vigna said the Luce's 2027 deliveries are "fully allocated." "If someone comes, he has to wait one year to get the cars," he told reporters, as TechCrunch reported.

Ferrari's problem in China cost it 105 cars. BMW's cost it about 38,000.

Why BMW can't

BMW is a premium carmaker, not a luxury one, and premium is a volume business. It needs hundreds of thousands of buyers to pay a little more for each car, and for a decade China supplied a large share of them. This quarter, BMW's sales there fell by about a quarter, which BMW says was in line with the overall market, and Chinese brands now take most of that market. They're down 21.8% over 9 months. There's no waiting list to backfill that.

BMW is doing several other things right. Its sales rose 2.5% in America and 2.6% in Europe, fully electric cars were 20% of its deliveries, and demand for the new Neue Klasse iX3 forced it to add production shifts. But US tariffs take about 1.25 percentage points off its margin this year, by its own estimate, and its restructuring and model cuts take about 2.5 points. A voluntary job-cut program for about 8,000 people, announced in July, filled within about 3 weeks of opening in October, and BMW is now extending it and cutting management faster. "We do not want to lose this momentum," Milan Nedeljković, its chief executive since May, said, according to Reuters. An analyst at Scope Ratings, Matthias Hellstern, was blunter: "I would not assume this is the end of the turbulence."

Volvo Cars showed the same pressure from a different angle. It sold 141,656 cars from August through October, 16% fewer than a year earlier, and said that in China it is "focusing on stabilizing the business rather than chasing volumes."

The same split, at auction

The collector market tells the same story in its own currency. The F50 that sold in Las Vegas on October 31 was the 334th of 349, delivered new in Britain. It brought $4.13 million more than the previous F50 record, which was only 2½ months old. In the same sale, a 2025 Daytona SP3 with 81 miles made $18,540,000. In London that day, another Daytona SP3, a former factory development car with 2,948 miles, brought £8.16 million, about $10.9 million. Same model, same day, and the difference of about $7.7 million came down mostly to miles and history.

Underneath those records, the market is flat. Hagerty's Market Rating was 58.73 in September, which Hagerty called "essentially flat," after hitting its lowest level in nearly 15 years in January, when it said "values are down across the board, on both high-end and broad-market vehicles." The median price at public auctions had fallen to $26,513, its lowest in nearly 6 years after inflation. At Monterey in August, a record $755.6 million changed hands, yet Hagerty found 1950s and 1960s cars selling below their condition values on average. "There is no rising tide here," it wrote.

What it means for everyone else

For the very wealthy, a rare car has become something closer to art: scarce, documented, barely used and priced on desire rather than on what it does. Ferrari builds new cars to that formula, and the auction houses sell old ones on it. Both are doing very well.

For everyone else, the middle of the market is where the pressure is. Premium carmakers are cutting jobs and models to protect thinner margins, and the collector cars most enthusiasts can afford, the ones that get driven, aren't rising with the headlines. That isn't bad news if you want to buy. The records are being set by a few dozen cars that will spend most of their lives under covers. The car you can actually drive on a Sunday hasn't been priced out of reach. It just isn't making anyone rich.

The short version

Ferrari sold 33 fewer cars this quarter and made 13% more money, because it builds fewer cars than buyers want and personalization is now more than a fifth of its car revenue. BMW, which needs volume, lost about a quarter of its China sales and now expects its car business to earn roughly nothing this year. At auction, the same split shows up: nearly unused modern Ferraris set records of more than $18 million while Hagerty calls the wider market flat.
