When the Recall Fix Doesn't Fix the Car

BMW is recalling the same 720,796 cars it recalled in 2024, because the first repair didn't cover the whole problem, and the new one isn't ready yet. How US recalls are supposed to work, what you're owed when one falls short, and what to do while you wait.

By Jolice Franklyn · 9 minute read

If you own a 2012–2016 BMW 328i, a 2013–2017 X3 or any of 12 other BMW lines from the 2012–2018 model years, NHTSA's advice, issued November 13, is blunt: "you should park your vehicle outside and away from buildings and other vehicles until the vehicle is repaired." A badly sealed connector on the water pump can let in moisture and short circuit, raising the risk of a fire even with the car parked and off. The catch: these are the same 720,796 cars BMW recalled for the same connector in 2024, and NHTSA counts about 10 incidents in the US, in cars that already had that repair, "that relate, or may relate" to the problem. The new remedy, NHTSA says, is "currently in development."

A recall is supposed to end with a safe car. When it doesn't, the law has rules for what happens next. Here's a practical guide to them, not legal advice.

What went wrong with the first fix

In 2024, BMW blamed blowby fluid from the crankcase ventilation system reaching the connector, and the fix was a shield to divert it, plus a new pump or connector where needed. That dealt with the fluid, not the connector. BMW's new filing says that over time, "due to material degradation at the connector," moisture could get into the pump anyway. BMW first put post-repair incidents, including a US 3 Series that smoldered at the connector, down to possibly improper repairs. A review in early 2026 found more. Lab work found the connector's plastic turning brittle in places, with cracks, and on October 28 BMW decided to recall the cars again.

Neither BMW nor NHTSA calls the incidents fires; BMW describes "overheating or thermal overload at the pump connector," and no crashes or injuries have been reported. It now estimates that fewer than 0.1% of the cars have the defect, against 1% in its 2024 filing.

How a recall is supposed to work

NHTSA's power to require recalls comes from the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act of 1966. Once a carmaker decides a defect is a safety risk, it has 5 working days to file a defect report, called a Part 573 report; BMW filed on November 4, the fifth working day after its decision. Owners must be told by first-class mail within 60 days of the filing, which for BMW means by January 3, 2027. If the fix isn't ready, they get an interim notice first and a second when it is. BMW's interim letters are expected to go out from December 25.

The repair must be free, and the carmaker picks the form: repair, replacement, or a refund of the price less reasonable depreciation. The free-remedy duty has an age limit: it doesn't cover a vehicle first bought more than 15 calendar years before the recall notice, or 5 years for a tire. The oldest cars here were built in early 2011, and BMW hasn't said how it will treat any past that line.

When the fix doesn't exist yet

A recall without a remedy is still a recall. The carmaker accepts the defect and owes you a free fix; the parts or procedure just aren't ready, so the interim advice is your protection. Checking has a catch, too. At NHTSA.gov/recalls, or in NHTSA's SaferCar app, a VIN or license plate shows open recalls, but these BMWs only become searchable on December 25, so a lookup before then may come back clean.

Who checks whether a fix worked

NHTSA can audit a recall through a Recall Query, which checks whether a recall reached owners, covered the right vehicles and has a repair that, in NHTSA's words, "appropriately addresses the safety risk." A new one opened on November 6, into 2,895 Waymo robotaxis. Waymo's software fix reached all 3,067 recalled vehicles by November 17, 2025, but NHTSA says "a majority of the incidents" it had gathered came after that date.

Second recalls aren't rare, either. GM's first fix for 2017–2019 Chevrolet Bolt EVs was interim software that capped charging at 90%; a 2021 recall replaced the battery modules. GM's new brake recall for the Cadillac Lyriq and other EVs, 26V717, folds in some Lyriqs from a 2024 recall, for a condition "not addressed by the remedy for recall 24V589." And NHTSA says some interim Takata air bag replacements "will eventually need to be replaced as well."

How many cars actually get fixed

A fix only works on cars that get it. BMW's 2024 repair letters went out from March 28, 2025. By the end of September 2026, its latest report to NHTSA shows, 291,682 cars had been repaired and 14,902 scrapped, stolen or exported, leaving 414,212 neither repaired nor removed from the count.

NHTSA's average completion rates for vehicle recalls from 2014 to 2024 range from 61.5% to 73.4%, though it warns that rates "should not be compared across recalls or manufacturers" and counts a vehicle's age among the factors. Carfax estimates that about 1 in 5 vehicles on the road still has an unfixed recall.

Buying used, or renting

Federal rules are stricter for rentals. Under the Raechel and Jacqueline Houck Safe Rental Car Act of 2015, a rental company with 35 or more vehicles can't rent, sell or lease a recalled car until it's fixed; if no fix exists yet, it may rent the car only after taking the carmaker's interim steps. Dealers face a similar limit, but the federal rule for them is written for new vehicles in stock, so don't assume a used car on a lot has had its recalls done. The Federal Trade Commission's advice is simple: "Enter the vehicle's VIN to find out if a vehicle is subject to an open recall."

If you paid for the repair yourself

A carmaker's reimbursement plan must generally cover owners who paid for the repair from a year before it notified NHTSA until at least 10 days after its last owner letter. It can ask for a receipt and exclude work the warranty would have covered, but it can't insist your repair matched its own, and it has 60 days to decide. NHTSA says it won't mediate disputes over eligibility or amounts. The federal requirement also stops at vehicles first bought more than 10 years before the notice, and most cars in this recall were built more than 10 years ago, so BMW's own plan matters. BMW's filing says owners who paid for the remedy before the recall notice "may be eligible for reimbursement according to BMW Group's reimbursement plan."

What to do now

  1. Park outside, away from buildings and other vehicles, until the car is repaired. It isn't a do-not-drive order.
  2. Confirm your car is included: call BMW at 1-800-525-7417 now, or check your VIN at NHTSA.gov/recalls from December 25. Cars that had the 2024 repair are included.
  3. Letters go to the registered owner, so update your registration if you've moved.
  4. Log any warning light, smell or smoke, with dates and photos, and keep every service record.
  5. Report problems to NHTSA at NHTSA.gov or on its Vehicle Safety Hotline, 888-327-4236. Complaints are where its defect screening starts.
  6. Keep receipts for any related repair you pay for.
  7. When the fix arrives, book it, and ask what the dealer offers while you wait; federal recall law doesn't require a loaner. If the car isn't adequately repaired within 60 days of you presenting it, the law treats that as evidence of unreasonable delay, unless NHTSA extends that period for good cause, and the carmaker can be obliged to replace the car or refund its price less depreciation.

The uncomfortable part isn't that BMW had to try again; it found the flaw in its own fix and filed. It's that more than half of these cars never got the first try.

The short version

BMW is recalling 720,796 cars from the 2012–2018 model years again, because its 2024 repair kept fluid off a water-pump connector but didn't stop the connector itself from degrading. The new fix isn't ready. Park outside, confirm your car with BMW now or by VIN from December 25, keep records and receipts, and report problems to NHTSA. Only 40.5% of these cars got the first repair, so check any used one.
