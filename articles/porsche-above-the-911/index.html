Fewer Cars, Higher Prices and a Supercar Above the 911: Porsche's New Future

The Mission S isn't simply another Porsche concept. It's the centerpiece of a plan to sell fewer cars, cut complexity and move the whole brand further beyond the reach of ordinary enthusiasts.

By Douglas James · 8 minute read

For most of its life, Porsche has treated the 911 as something close to sacred. Other flagships came along, the 959, the Carrera GT and the 918 Spyder, but they were visitors. They arrived, showed what Porsche could do, became instant collectibles and left without disturbing the order of things. The 911 stayed at the center of Porsche's world. The Mission S changes that.

Porsche revealed the Mission S on October 15 on a pier in Queens, New York, looking across the water to Manhattan. It's the spot where the first photos of the 550 Spyder were taken in 1953. Porsche calls it a vision model rather than a concept, and it previews a mid-engined super sports car platform that Porsche means to keep above the 911 for good. Michael Leiters, Porsche's chief executive, said it comes "not as a one-off hypercar," but as "a permanent and versatile addition to our customer offering." That alone would be big news. But the Mission S matters much more when you put it beside Porsche's new corporate strategy. It isn't an isolated design exercise. It's the most visible symbol of a company preparing to become smaller, leaner, more exclusive and considerably more expensive.

The 911 is no longer the ceiling

The Mission S borrows openly from Porsche history. Its flat front takes its cue from the 911 GT1, the roofline beneath its active rear wing recalls the 959, its cooling echoes the radiator fan of the 917, its round headlights and fenders are pure 911, and its wheels borrow the dial design of the 928. Inside, Porsche went the other way from most new cars: 5 round gauges with an analog rev-counter needle, real switches and push-pull controls, and no permanently fitted center screen at all, just a retractable holder for your phone. More important than the styling is where the car sits. Porsche says the production car will start a model line above the 911, and that changes what the 911 means without making it any less important. The 911 becomes Porsche's core sports car rather than its absolute summit. That gives Porsche room to push the most desirable 911 versions further upmarket, while the Mission S competes in a different financial and emotional class, against Ferrari, Lamborghini and McLaren.

Porsche hasn't named the engine, the output or the price, saying only that the engine sits in the middle. Leiters told Auto Express the platform can take combustion, hybrid and plug-in hybrid power, but not batteries alone: "There won't be a BEV on that platform: there is no demand." Reports of a flat-eight engine and a price around €500,000 (about $560,000), which came from the German business paper Handelsblatt in September, remain unconfirmed. What Porsche has confirmed is the business philosophy behind it: value over volume.

Porsche is preparing to sell fewer cars

A week before the reveal, on October 7, Porsche laid out a new strategy it calls Sportwagenschmiede '35, roughly "sports car forge '35." It wants to break even at fewer than 200,000 vehicles a year. For context, it delivered 279,449 in 2025, down 10% from 2024 and well below its 2023 peak of 320,221. China has been the biggest problem: Porsche delivered 41,938 cars there last year, down 26%, and barely half of what it sold there in 2023. Its group operating margin fell to 1.1% in 2025, from 14.1% the year before.

So Porsche is trying to make more from each car instead of counting on growth. It wants to raise the average selling price of its top-of-the-range models by about 20% in the medium term. Reuters reported that the target covers its most expensive 10,000 or so vehicles, and the Financial Times put it at roughly €270,000 (about $302,000) today, rising to more than €330,000 (about $370,000) by the end of the decade. New models will come mainly in what Porsche calls the D and E segments, the upper end of its range, where the margins are highest, and Porsche wants sales through Sonderwunsch, its one-off personalization program, to grow sixfold.

That doesn't mean every 911 is about to cost 20% more. The target is the average price of Porsche's most expensive cars, and it can get there with more GT models, limited editions, expensive factory options, one-off commissions and the new Mission S line. But the direction is unmistakable: Porsche wants its most desirable cars to cost more, carry more options and earn more from every buyer. Patrick Hummel, an analyst at UBS, put it plainly to the Financial Times: "It's replicating what Ferrari has been successful at."

Fewer variants, not necessarily fewer identities

Porsche also wants about 20% fewer model variants, and the distinction matters. It hasn't said 1 in 5 model lines will disappear. It's cutting the overlapping engines, body styles and trim combinations that cost money to engineer, certify, build and market, and it wants each remaining variant to sell about 30% more. Fewer configurations should cut costs, simplify inventory and make each version easier to understand.

The danger is that the unusual versions enthusiasts remember often exist because Porsche was willing to build so many. A leaner configurator may be good for margins, but it could also lose some of the odd, narrow-purpose cars that give the brand its character. Porsche's answer is more "highly emotive" 911 derivatives and, by 2030, at least 1 brand-defining new product every year. In other words, the everyday complexity shrinks while the expensive special-car business grows.

The Ferrari strategy, with Porsche scale

The comparison with Ferrari is unavoidable. Ferrari has mastered scarcity, personalization and the careful movement of customers through ever more exclusive tiers. Porsche has always occupied a broader space. It sells genuine sports cars, but it also sells enough SUVs to pay for engineering programs that would otherwise be impossible. Now Porsche seems to want some of Ferrari's economics without giving up all of Porsche's scale. The Macan and Cayenne will keep providing volume, while the 911, the special versions and the Mission S bring in bigger margins and more prestige.

That could work. Porsche's brand is strong enough that restricting access makes an expensive object feel more desirable, and its personalization business has plenty of room to grow. Buyers already accept extraordinary option prices, because a Porsche configured personally feels more meaningful than one taken off a dealer's lot. But there's a risk in deliberately manufacturing exclusivity after decades of growth.

What happens to the attainable Porsche?

Porsche became powerful not only because wealthy collectors wanted its rarest cars, but because generations of enthusiasts believed they might own one someday. A used Boxster, Cayman, 944 or older 911 made the mythology reachable. The gap between admiring and owning was big, but it wasn't impossible.

The electric successors to the 718 Boxster and Cayman are still coming, with 2028 their first full year of production, and Porsche has said no new combustion version is planned. Production of the combustion 718 ended in October 2025, which leaves an obvious hole at the bottom of the range. If the new entry-level sports cars are expensive, heavily optioned EVs, the cheapest way into a new Porsche sports car could get a lot harder to reach. The company faces a delicate balance. More exclusivity can make the cars more desirable, but too much distance can turn a living enthusiast community into an audience watching through showroom glass.

The Mission S is beautiful precisely because it looks backward while Porsche moves forward. It borrows from the cars that built the mythology Porsche now wants to earn more from. The question isn't whether Porsche can charge more. It almost certainly can. The question is whether Porsche can become more exclusive without abandoning the enthusiasts who made exclusivity worth something in the first place.

The short version

Porsche revealed the Mission S on October 15, a vision model for a permanent line of mid-engined super sports cars above the 911, with no engine, output, price or date yet. A week earlier it set out a plan to break even below 200,000 cars a year, raise the average price of its top models by about 20% and cut its model variants by about 20%. Porsche can almost certainly charge more. The question is whether it can do it without losing the enthusiasts who made the brand worth paying for.
