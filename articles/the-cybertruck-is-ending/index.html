The Cybertruck Is Ending. Was That Tesla's Failure or the Electric Pickup's?

Tesla promised a $39,900 truck (about $52,600 today) and a quarter of a million sales a year. It listed one from $60,990 (about $67,400 today), sold about 20,000 last year, and the other big electric pickups did even worse. What went wrong for the truck, and what went wrong for the idea.

By Sloane Whitaker · 7 minute read

When the Cybertruck appeared in November 2019, it looked like nothing else on the road, and that was the point. Its stainless-steel body was flat, sharp and impossible to ignore, its price was supposed to start at $39,900 (about $52,600 in today's money) and Elon Musk called it "our best product ever." For a few years, it was the most talked-about vehicle in the world.

On November 5, Pacific time, Musk said Tesla will wind down Cybertruck production at its Texas factory around the middle of next year "to make room for increased Cybercab production." Giga Texas is the only plant that builds the truck. 2 days later, he said the line would be taken apart "around June/July next year." The easy reading is that the Cybertruck failed. The more useful question is what failed: the truck, or the idea of a big, expensive electric pickup.

What was promised

The 2019 promise was a truck from $39,900, in production by 2021. By October 2023, a month before deliveries began, Musk was talking about volume. "I think we'll end up with roughly 0.25 million Cybertrucks a year," he said on Tesla's earnings call, adding that Tesla would probably reach that rate "sometime in 2025," and that "over 1 million people" had reserved one. On the same call, he said: "we dug our own grave with the Cybertruck," warning of "enormous challenges in reaching volume production."

The truck that arrived on November 30, 2023, started at $60,990 (about $67,400 today) for a rear-drive version that wasn't delivered at that price, $79,990 for all-wheel drive (about $88,500 today) and $99,990 for the Cyberbeast (about $110,600). This month, after the wind-down was announced, Tesla raised prices again and stopped taking orders for the cheapest version. The least expensive Cybertruck you can order now is $92,490.

What happened

Kelley Blue Book estimates Americans bought 38,965 Cybertrucks in 2024, 20,237 in 2025 and 11,586 in the first 9 months of this year. Last year's total was about 8% of the 250,000 a year Musk had talked about. The truck has had 11 recalls, including 46,096 trucks in March 2025 for an exterior panel that could come off, and 63,619 that October for parking lights that were too bright. It never went on sale in the European Union, mainly because of pedestrian-safety rules, it's built only with the steering wheel on the left, and last month Tesla canceled it for Australia and New Zealand after 7 years of reservations.

There was another sign of weak demand. Musk's own companies bought 1,339 Cybertrucks in the fourth quarter of 2025, about 19% of the trucks registered in America that quarter, according to S&P Global Mobility data reported by Electrek. SpaceX alone bought 1,279.

The Cybertruck is still America's best-selling electric pickup. It's the best seller in a class that shrank by half.

The rest of the class did worse

Here's the part that complicates the obvious story. Even falling, the Cybertruck is still America's best-selling electric pickup, and by a wide margin. In the first 9 months of this year, it outsold the Chevrolet Silverado EV more than 2 to 1.

Ford's chief executive, Jim Farley, explained the problem bluntly last December, when Ford ended the all-electric Lightning: "the very high-end EVs, the $50K, 70K, $80,000 vehicles, they just weren't selling." Stellantis canceled the battery-electric Ram 1500 "as demand for full-size battery-electric trucks slows in North America." GM says it hasn't canceled any electric trucks, but its plant has cut shifts and stood idle.

So whose failure was it?

Both, but mostly the category's. The market for a full-size electric pickup priced like a luxury car turned out to be much smaller than every carmaker thought. Pickup buyers pay for capability they can count on: towing, long trips, cold mornings far from a charger. An electric truck that costs $70,000 to $100,000 asks them to pay more for less certainty, and most didn't. The Cybertruck outsold its rivals partly because it was the one people wanted to be seen in.

But Tesla made its own version of the problem harder. It designed a truck that couldn't be sold in Europe or in right-hand-drive countries, and launched it at more than 50% above the price it first promised. Then it raised prices as demand fell. A design that made the Cybertruck famous also made it a harder sell to the ordinary truck buyers who keep the F-150 and the Silverado at the top of the sales charts.

What Tesla gets from ending it

Musk's explanation is about resources, not sales. "There are a very limited number of smart, dedicated people," he posted, and "we can't use those people or factory space and machinery for scaling Cybercab." Tesla lists the Cybercab's capacity in Texas at more than 125,000 a year, the same as the Cybertruck's, and has been giving rides in it in Austin since September 3. The truck's lead engineer, Wes Morrill, called it "an incredible technology development platform" that "lives on through Cybercab." Its 48-volt electrical system, steer-by-wire and wiring architecture all carried over. In that sense, the Cybertruck paid for parts of a car Tesla thinks matters more.

For owners, Tesla says parts and service will continue. "Yes, of course! Tesla will maintain spare parts and service will continue support," Morrill wrote.

What the electric pickup becomes next

The next round of electric pickups looks nothing like the last. Ford's Fathom, due in 2027, is a midsize truck priced from $28,350 plus a $1,595 destination charge, with cheaper LFP batteries. The next F-150 Lightning will carry a gasoline engine as a generator, which Ford estimates will give it more than 700 miles of range. Ram has gone the same way. The lesson the industry took isn't that people don't want electric trucks. It's that they won't pay luxury prices for a truck that can't promise to get them all the way there.

The short version

The Cybertruck missed Musk's forecast by about 92% and leaves production in Texas next summer, but it's still America's best-selling electric pickup, because the whole full-size electric pickup class shrank by about half this year. The category failed first: buyers wouldn't pay $70,000 to $100,000 for an electric truck. Tesla made its own version harder, with a design that limited where it could be sold and a price far above its promise. The next electric pickups are smaller, cheaper or carry a gasoline generator.
