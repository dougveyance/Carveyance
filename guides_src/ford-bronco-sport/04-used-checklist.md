A used Bronco Sport Big Bend and a Badlands share a silhouette but not their abilities. Most have the 1.5-liter 3-cylinder, and the Badlands has the 2.0-liter with more off-road hardware. Before you talk about price, confirm the engine, trim and packages from the VIN, the original window sticker and the service records. A dealer's "fully loaded 4x4" isn't an equipment list.

## The fuel-injector recall can't be waved away

Certain 2021–2024 Bronco Sports with the 1.5-liter are covered by recall 25V467, because a fuel injector can crack and leak fuel into the engine compartment, which can cause a fire. The remedy is new engine software plus a new high-pressure fuel rail and injectors. This campaign expanded on earlier ones (22V859, 24V187 and 25V165), so a seller's statement that "the injector recall was done" may refer to an older, incomplete fix. Get the VIN, check the current status with Ford and NHTSA, and make sure the completed work matches the latest campaign. Ask for the paperwork, and settle any pending parts or appointments before you take delivery.

## How the 1.5 drives matters more than its cylinder count

At a cold start, listen for unusual noises and watch the idle, the warning lights and the exhaust. On the road, check for smooth gear engagement, predictable throttle response and a steady temperature gauge. A 3-cylinder naturally feels different from a 4-cylinder, but a stumble, a persistent misfire or an engine warning isn't "just what they do." Smell for fuel under the hood. On a Badlands, check the drive modes and rear drive unit as the owner's manual describes, and don't let the seller demonstrate them on dry pavement in a way that stresses the parts.

## The underbody tells the truth

Look at the skid protection, the lower bumper corners, the tires, the wheels, the wheel bearings and the suspension. Gravel scratches aren't serious in themselves, but big impacts, replaced suspension arms, damaged underbody wiring or active leaks need a closer look. On a 4x4 the tires should match in brand, size and wear, because mismatched tires stress the drive system. Look for signs of water around the carpet edges and seat mounts.

## The practical things buyers overlook

Test the flip-up rear glass, the liftgate struts, the weather seals, the USB ports, the touchscreen, the heated features, the cameras, the climate control and every key. Check the cargo floor for damp and the tie-downs for damage. On a 2026, don't assume it has a wireless charger or a garage opener. If it has a roof rack or a hitch, check Ford's limits for that exact configuration.

## Recalls: check by VIN

- **2021–2022:** 22V191, an engine oil separator that can crack and leak on the 1.5-liter. 21V625, a fuel delivery module that can leak inside the tank. 21V922, rear brake linings that may have been made incorrectly.
- **2021–2023:** 25V019, a 12-volt battery that can degrade and fail suddenly.
- **2021–2024:** 25V467, the 1.5-liter fuel-injector recall described above. 24V267 and its follow-up 25V158, body and powertrain modules that may not detect a weak 12-volt battery.
- **2025:** 25V372, a cylinder head that may have been made incorrectly. 25V049, an instrument cluster that may not light up at start-up.
- **2025–2026:** 25V788, the instrument cluster failing at start-up. 26V201, a seat sensor that may not correctly detect a passenger for the air bags.
- **2021–2026:** 26V340, front lower control arm ball joints that may have been installed or repaired incorrectly at the plant, letting the arm disconnect from the knuckle. Ford tells owners of affected vehicles not to drive them until it's fixed.
- **Every year:** run the VIN at [NHTSA's recall lookup](https://www.nhtsa.gov/recalls) and ask for proof of each repair. A recall for a model year doesn't mean every vehicle is affected.

## Walk away if

The seller won't give you the VIN, or the car has unexplained accident repairs, a fuel smell, water damage, unmatched tires, persistent driveline vibration, several undocumented modifications or an open safety recall. Leave room in the budget for tires and maintenance, and don't assume a compact SUV makes every repair cheap.

We don't publish average prices. They go stale quickly, and an average that mixes a 2021 Base with a 2026 Badlands tells you very little. Compare 20 to 30 listings for the same years, engine, trim and package, with the same mileage and title history, and keep dealer asking prices separate from what cars actually sold for. [FitDrive's deal check](/fitdrive/) does the first part for you. Cross-shop a Subaru Forester or a Toyota RAV4 of the same age, and take the cost of tires and any open recall work off the price.

Buying from a private seller, or keeping the Bronco Sport past its factory warranty? Service plan providers to compare include [Chaiz](https://www.chaiz.com), [Endurance](https://www.endurancewarranty.com) and [Olive](https://www.olive.com). Carveyance doesn't rank or recommend them. Read what each one covers and excludes, especially for the turbocharged engine, the transmission and the 4-wheel-drive system, and see the [FTC's guidance on auto service contracts](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2023/03/what-know-about-auto-service-contracts-extended-warranty-scams) first.

## Our shortlist

- **Best value:** a 2023–2024 Big Bend with documented service and the latest injector remedy done.
- **Best all-rounder:** a 2025 Big Bend or Outer Banks, with the bigger screens and the wireless charger.
- **Best for trails:** a Badlands, ideally from 2025 on with the Sasquatch Package.
- **Cheapest way in:** a 2021–2022 Base or Big Bend with every recall done and proof of it.
