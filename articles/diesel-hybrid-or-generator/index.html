Diesel, Hybrid, or Generator?

General Motors, Ford and Ram have stopped agreeing on what a pickup should burn. The answers arrive in the same month diesel hit a record $6.52 a gallon, which makes one of these bets look very brave.

By Elliot Vance · 9 minute read

For most of the modern pickup era, the Detroit three argued about the same four numbers: horsepower, torque, payload and towing. The trucks were different in detail and identical in philosophy.

That has quietly ended. General Motors, Ford and Ram now disagree about something more basic than output. They disagree about what should be in the tank.

GM is keeping diesel and adding a bigger tank. Ford dropped its light-duty diesel and bet on a conventional hybrid. Ram is building a truck that carries a gasoline engine purely to make electricity. Three companies, three answers, one customer base that does not care how elegant a technology sounds if it cannot pull a trailer across a state.

And then, in the same month, the price of diesel did something none of them planned for.

GM: make the known quantity go farther

On September 17, GM detailed the 2027 Chevrolet Silverado 1500 and GMC Sierra 1500. The 3.0-liter Duramax inline-six carries over at 305 hp and 495 lb-ft, with a 12,100-pound tow rating, and it gains revised pistons, a new turbo vane design, a next-generation NOx sensor and a diesel exhaust fluid tank 37% larger than before. Two new V-8s arrive alongside it, a 5.7-liter and a 6.6-liter, both grown out of the old 5.3 and 6.2 by a longer stroke.

The headline, though, is a fuel tank. An optional 34-gallon (129-liter) tank is available on the diesel and both V-8s, and at GM's estimated 27 mpg highway for a two-wheel-drive Duramax, that works out to 918 miles. GM says "more than 900."

Read the asterisk

That number needs two things at once: the optional tank and two-wheel drive. A four-wheel-drive Duramax keeps the 24-gallon tank and a 26-mpg highway rating, which lands closer to 624 miles. The engine's efficiency did not change. The range is the tank.

That is not a criticism so much as a description. GM's fuel capacity had become a genuine weak point: a 24-gallon tank behind an engine returning 13 to 15 mpg while towing leaves roughly 300 miles between stops, which is how a 2,000-mile towing test turns into a fuel-stop scavenger hunt. The new tank puts GM ahead of the Ram 1500's 33 gallons and the Tundra's 32.2, though still behind the F-150's 36.

GM President Mark Reuss framed the choice as simplicity, arguing that the diesel is a more direct route to efficiency than "adding a hybrid system to an existing powertrain." Crew-cab production begins in October at Fort Wayne, Indiana, and Silao, Mexico.

Ford: make the electrification invisible

Ford went the other way. It discontinued the light-duty F-150 diesel and put its weight behind the PowerBoost hybrid, and the appeal was never only fuel economy. A conventional hybrid sharpens low-speed response, recovers braking energy, and, through Pro Power Onboard, turns the truck into a generator for tools, a campsite or a house during an outage.

The philosophical difference matters more than the mpg. Ford asks the owner to change nothing. Put gasoline in it, drive it like an F-150, and the electrification happens in the background whether the owner thinks about it or not.

The compromise is that a conventional hybrid reduces fuel use rather than replacing it, and its advantage narrows exactly where trucks work hardest. Hybrids recover energy from braking. A truck towing at 70 mph on an interstate does very little braking.

Ram: turn the EV inside out

Ram's answer is the strangest and, on paper, the most interesting. An extended-range electric truck drives its wheels with electric motors and carries a gasoline engine purely as an onboard generator, firing up once the battery needs support. Ram is targeting roughly 690 miles between stops.

In theory it collects the best of both: the instant, controllable torque of electric drive, and refueling that takes five minutes at any gas station. An owner could commute on grid electricity all week, then tow 400 miles on Saturday without once queueing at a charger.

That last point is not a small one. Public charging is inconvenient for a full-size pickup and close to hostile for one pulling a trailer, because charging bays are laid out like parking stalls rather than fuel lanes. Many require unhitching. Ram's layout sidesteps the problem entirely.

It also means carrying nearly every major component of both kinds of vehicle: a large battery, electric motors, a gasoline engine, a fuel system and the control hardware to blend them. The result could be remarkably capable or remarkably heavy, expensive and complicated, and nobody will know which until it is rated, priced and towed.

And then the pump moved

GM announced its 900-mile diesel in the same month diesel became the most expensive fuel in the country by a distance nobody forecast.

A gallon of diesel reached $6.52 on Wednesday, according to AAA, up 73% from $3.77 before the war in Iran began. In California the state average was around $7.70 in early September. Gasoline has risen too, to roughly $4.15, but nothing like as far.

The causes sit on the supply side rather than the demand side. The closure of the Strait of Hormuz took about 20% of the world's oil off the market, and while some has returned, it is nowhere near prewar levels. Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries have removed a further 800,000 to 1 million barrels a day of diesel, according to S&P Global Energy. Refiners have an incentive to make jet fuel, whose price has also climbed. Distillate inventories fell to roughly 103 million barrels by late August, the lowest for that point in the year since 1951. The United States uses 4.1 million barrels of diesel a day and exports 1.5 million.

A year ago the three powertrains sat within four cents of each other per mile, and the diesel's extra range and torque came almost free. Today the diesel costs roughly nine cents a mile more than the hybrid. Over 20,000 miles that is about $1,800 a year, which is real money to a contractor and merely irritating to a weekend hauler.

The picture changes again with a trailer behind you. Towing collapses the hybrid's advantage, because there is little braking energy to recover at highway speed and a great deal of aerodynamic drag to overcome. Diesel's low-rpm torque and steady-state efficiency are exactly suited to the job, and the 34-gallon tank turns a fuel-stop problem into a non-problem. The same truck that looks expensive on the school run looks sensible pulling 10,000 pounds to Denver.

Who each truck is actually for

The honest answer is that none of these is the right truck, because there is no such thing.

The diesel is for the person who covers enormous distances, tows heavy and often, or works where charging is theoretical and the nearest station is 60 miles away. Range and torque are worth paying for when the alternative is a lost afternoon.

The hybrid is for the broad middle: mixed driving, occasional towing, no interest in plugging anything in, and real value in a truck that powers a jobsite or a house.

The range-extender is for the owner who can charge at home, drives short distances most days, and refuses to accept a compromise on the four weekends a year when the trailer comes out.

Put each in the wrong hands and the conclusions invert. A diesel that only does five-mile town trips never reaches the operating conditions it was built for. A plug-in truck that never gets plugged in is a very heavy gasoline truck carrying an expensive battery. A hybrid towing at 75 mph delivers a fraction of the advantage its city rating implies.

What GM is really betting on

It would be easy to read the timing as a blunder. It is not. A truck program is locked in years before it reaches a showroom, and the 2027 Silverado was engineered long before any of these prices existed. GM did not choose diesel in September 2026; it chose diesel around 2022 and is now living with the consequences, good and bad.

What GM is betting on is the customer, not the commodity. The Silverado and Sierra are its best-selling vehicles worldwide and produce the bulk of its profit, and the diesel buyer inside that group is specific: the contractor, the farmer, the fleet manager, the person who tows for a living and has bought compression ignition for 30 years because nothing else does the job the same way. That buyer does not change powertrains over a bad quarter at the pump. He complains, he adjusts his rates, and he buys another diesel.

Ford and Ram are chasing the buyer they might win. GM is holding the one it already has.

The question the next two years will answer is not whether GM guessed right about fuel prices. Nobody guessed right about fuel prices. It is whether a working-truck customer's loyalty survives a spike of this size, and whether the extra ten gallons in the tank feels like a gift or a bigger bill at every fill.

Our own view is that the only way to settle any of this is to run all three on the same route: city errands, a long freeway leg, a mountain grade and a real trailer, measuring fuel, electricity, and the time spent standing still. Until somebody does that, everything above is arithmetic, and arithmetic is not the same as a tow hook.
