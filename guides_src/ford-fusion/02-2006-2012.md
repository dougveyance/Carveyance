The first Fusion was Ford admitting it needed help: the CD3 platform underneath came from Mazda's G architecture, and the car shared its Hermosillo line with the Mercury Milan and Lincoln MKZ. What Ford added was size, a soft-touch American cabin and value pricing. The 2006–2009 cars — 160-hp 2.3, 221-hp 3.0 V6, optional AWD — were competent rather than memorable, but the chassis was honest and the sales line only went up.

## The 2010 refresh is the one to buy

For 2010 everything improved at once: a 175-hp 2.5 base engine, the V6 up to 240 hp, a 6-speed automatic across the range, and 2 new flagships — the 263-hp 3.5 Fusion Sport and the 191-hp, 41-mpg-city Hybrid. Motor Trend handed the lineup its 2010 Car of the Year award, and the Hybrid separately took North American Car of the Year. On today's used market the refresh cars cost barely more than the early ones, which makes a 2010–2012 SEL or Sport the clear pick of the generation.

## What these cost, and what to accept

This is beater-budget territory now — the cheapest Fusions are 4-figure cars, and even nice late V6s rarely crack 5 figures. At those prices, condition is everything and options are nothing: buy the best-maintained example in reach, prefer the 6-speed cars, and treat any transmission hesitation on early 5-speed autos as the warning it is. The checklist chapter covers the specifics, but the headline is simple — these were built in high volume to a proven recipe, and a cared-for one is as cheap as dependable transportation gets.
