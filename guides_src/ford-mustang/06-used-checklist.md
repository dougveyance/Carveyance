Start with the car's identity, not its stripes. Decode the VIN and ask for the original window sticker or build sheet. A GT badge and an appearance package don't tell you whether the car has the Performance Pack, the bigger brakes, MagneRide or the active exhaust. On a Bullitt, Mach 1, Shelby, Dark Horse or GTD, confirm it's genuine through Ford's documentation. Modifications, especially superchargers, tunes, removed emissions parts and lowered suspension, change both the risk and the value.

## Under the car and around it

Look for mismatched paint, overspray and signs of crash repair; Mustangs attract enthusiastic owners and sometimes less careful body shops. Check the age and wear of all 4 tires, inspect the wheels for curb and pothole damage, and look at the brake pads and rotors. On a lift, look at the rear subframe, the differential and the suspension arms for bends, leaks or scrape marks. On a convertible, work the top several times and check the seals and rear footwells for water.

## The road test

Start it cold and listen for ticks, rattles or a rough idle. On a manual, check for notchy shifts, grinding, clutch slip and a pedal that sticks, both cold and hot. On the 10-speed automatic, look for harsh or confused shifts and ask whether the transmission software has been updated. Listen for clunks from the suspension and whine or clunks from the differential, and make sure the steering returns to center on its own. Have a shop read the stored trouble codes rather than trusting a dashboard with no warning lights.

## Track use

A Mustang that has seen track days isn't automatically a bad buy, but a poorly maintained one is. Ask for brake fluid changes, differential fluid changes, tire invoices and any evidence of overheating. On a Shelby GT350 or GT500, a Mach 1 or a Dark Horse, budget for a specialist inspection; on carbon-fiber wheels, superchargers and rare performance parts, repair bills can outweigh the price difference between 2 cars. Get an insurance quote before you make an offer.

## Recalls: check by VIN

- **2015–2017:** 25V614, front seat belt pretensioner cables that can corrode; 332,778 cars. 22V082, rearview camera wiring that can blank the image; 330,784 cars.
- **2020 automatics:** 20V573, a brake pedal bracket that can fracture in a sudden stop.
- **2020–2023:** 23V727, a brake fluid level sensor that may not warn when the fluid is low; 187,922 cars. 25V689 covers cars repaired incorrectly.
- **2022–2023:** 24V493, a steering torque sensor that may not be calibrated, which can turn the wheel against the driver's input. 25V096 covers cars repaired incorrectly.
- **2024:** 24V593, rear suspension knuckles with a casting defect that can fail. 24V394, a clutch line that can touch the exhaust and melt.
- **2024–2026:** 26V547, engine-bay wiring ground connections that can fracture, which can cut drive power or the lights, wipers or air conditioning; 148,663 cars.
- **Every year:** run the VIN at [NHTSA's recall lookup](https://www.nhtsa.gov/recalls) and with Ford, and ask for proof each repair was done. A recall for a model year doesn't mean every car is affected, and an empty list online is never proof a VIN is clear.

## Documents to ask for

Ask for the maintenance history with dates and mileage, the window sticker or build sheet, all the keys, tire and brake invoices, records of any track days, and receipts for modifications and who installed them. On a Shelby, a Bullitt, a Mach 1 or a GTD, keep the original paperwork with the car; it's part of the value.

## Walk away if

The car has a branded title or flood damage, a supercharger or tune nobody can document, a transmission that slips or bangs into gear, signs of frame or subframe repair, or a seller who won't allow an independent inspection.

We don't publish average prices. They go stale quickly, and an average that mixes a 2017 EcoBoost with a 2020 GT500 tells you very little.
