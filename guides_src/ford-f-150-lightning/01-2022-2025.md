The Lightning's genius was its refusal to be special. Ford took the standard SuperCrew cab and 5.5-foot bed, swapped the engine for 2 motors and the frame rails' empty space for battery, and sold the result as just another F-150 that happened to hit 60 mph in 4.0 seconds. Car and Driver's test truck ran the quarter mile in 12.7 seconds, dead level with a Mustang Mach 1, while carrying plywood. Every Lightning is all-wheel drive, every Lightning makes 775 lb-ft, and the only drivetrain decision that matters is the battery, covered in the next chapter.

## The trims

The **Pro** is the work truck: vinyl floors, the smaller screen, and the same torque as the $90,000 version. Fleet buyers got most of them, which means used examples run high miles but simple lives. The **XLT** adds the consumer basics and is the volume used buy. The **Lariat** brings leather, the 15.5-inch portrait screen and most of the luxury without Platinum pricing. The **Platinum** gets the extended-range battery as standard, massaging seats and 22-inch wheels that cost it 20 miles of EPA range. The late **Flash** trim of 2024–25 slotted between XLT and Lariat with the big battery and the big screen, and is a quiet sweet spot when you find one.

## What it's like

Lower and heavier than a gas F-150, with the battery in the floor, the Lightning corners flatter than any 3-ton truck should and rides better unloaded than a leaf-sprung gas truck ever has, thanks to the independent rear suspension the battery demanded. The frunk, 400 liters of lockable, drained, powered storage where the V8 used to live, turns out to be the feature owners mention first. Payload and towing are real, up to 4,536 kg (10,000 lb) of trailer with the extended-range battery and the tow package, but range falls fast with a trailer attached, and that trade is the honest heart of the next chapter.

## Why it died, and why that's your opening

Ford built the Rouge Electric Vehicle Center to make 150,000 Lightnings a year; it never needed half of that. Pandemic costs pushed prices up 30–40 percent from the $39,974 launch Pro, demand cooled industry-wide, and in December 2025 Ford ended production and pivoted to a range-extended successor. Sales still grew every single year, 15,617 to 24,165 to 33,570, with 27,307 in the final year, outselling the Tesla Cybertruck. About 100,000 trucks exist, Ford must support them for years, and the used market has priced in the obituary. For a buyer who understands the next chapter, that spells bargain.
