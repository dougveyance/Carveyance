The Clarity Plug-in Hybrid went on sale on December 1, 2017, at $33,400 ($44,700 in 2026 dollars), or $36,600 ($49,000) for the Touring. It was the only Clarity sold across the whole country. When new, buyers could claim a federal tax credit of up to $7,500 ($10,000 in 2026 dollars).

## How it works

[[chart:range]]

It's a plug-in hybrid: a 17-kWh battery, charged from the grid, drives a 181-hp electric motor, and when the battery runs low a 1.5-liter Atkinson-cycle 4-cylinder takes over, mostly generating electricity for the motor and sometimes driving the wheels directly. Together they make 212 hp, and the motor makes 232 lb-ft of torque.

The EPA rated it at 47 miles on electricity alone, which Honda said was the most of any plug-in hybrid sedan at the time, and 340 miles in all with a full tank of gas. On electricity it's rated at 110 MPGe, and once the battery is low it's rated at 42 mpg on gasoline. Car and Driver ran a 2018 Touring to 60 mph in 7.7 seconds.

## Charging

Honda quoted 2.5 hours for a full charge from a 240-volt Level 2 charger and about 12 hours from a 120-volt household outlet, using the cable that came with the car. A Level 2 charger at home refills an empty battery in an evening; an ordinary outlet takes about half a day.

The 47-mile figure is for a new car in EPA test conditions. Cold weather, heavy use of the heater, high speeds and an aging battery all reduce it, and the range shown on the dashboard is a prediction based on recent driving, not a measure of the battery's health.

## Inside

It's a big car for a plug-in hybrid: Honda built the Clarity as a midsize sedan with room for 5, with the battery under the passenger compartment, so the trunk still holds 15.5 cubic feet. The rear seat folds 60/40 for long loads, but the opening is a sedan's. Every one has an 8-inch touchscreen with Apple CarPlay and Android Auto; the Touring adds leather, power front seats and navigation.

## Who it suits

The Plug-in Hybrid makes most sense for a driver who can charge at home and whose usual day is within its electric range, with longer trips on gasoline. If you can't charge where you park, or most of your driving is long highway runs, you'd be carrying a heavy battery you rarely use, and an ordinary hybrid would make more sense.

## Which one to buy

Any year is the same car: the Plug-in Hybrid barely changed from 2018 to 2021. The Touring is worth a little more if you want leather, power seats and navigation. Choose on the condition of the car, the charging test and the recall records rather than the year, and check that 2018–2019 cars have had the fuel pump recall done.
