Most Camry shoppers start with a year and a budget. The better place to start is the powertrain, because it decides how the car feels, what it costs to run and, in the V6's case, whether you'll ever find another one quite like it.

## The engines

|  | 2018–2024 4-cylinder | 2018–2024 V6 | 2018–2024 hybrid | 2025–2026 hybrid |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Engine | 2.5-liter 4-cylinder | 3.5-liter V6 | 2.5-liter 4-cylinder with electric motors | 2.5-liter 4-cylinder with electric motors |
| Transmission | 8-speed automatic | 8-speed automatic | Electronically controlled CVT | Electronically controlled CVT |
| Power | 203 hp (206 XSE) | 301 hp | 208 hp | 225 hp, 232 hp AWD |
| EPA combined, best version | 32 mpg | 26 mpg | 52 mpg | 51 mpg |
| All-wheel drive | 2020–2024, most grades | Not offered | Not offered | Every grade |
| 0–60 mph, Car and Driver | 7.9 sec (SE) | 5.8 sec (XSE) | 7.9 sec (XLE) | 6.8 sec (XSE AWD) |

[[chart:power]]

The V6 is in a different league for power, and the newest hybrid closes part of the gap. The older hybrid makes about as much power as the 4-cylinder, and in Car and Driver's testing a 2018 XLE Hybrid matched a 4-cylinder SE to 60 mph, at 7.9 seconds each. It was quicker where it counts on the highway, though, needing 5.1 seconds to go from 50 to 70 mph against the SE's 6.0.

## The cost of driving

[[chart:mpg]]

At 12,000 miles a year and $4 a gallon, a 32-mpg 4-cylinder uses about $1,500 of fuel a year. A 26-mpg V6 uses about $1,846, and a 52-mpg Hybrid LE about $923. That's roughly $346 a year more for the V6 than the 4-cylinder, and $577 a year less for the hybrid.

[[chart:fuelcost]]

These are examples, not forecasts. Your miles, your mix of city and highway driving and your local gas price will move them. Insurance, tires, financing and resale value will move the total cost of ownership even more. The hybrid's advantage is largest in traffic and smallest on long, steady highway runs.

## Choose the 4-cylinder if

You want the simplest, cheapest Camry to buy. The 2018–2024 4-cylinder has a conventional 8-speed automatic, plenty of power for daily driving and the widest choice of grades and prices. It's also the only way to get the 2020–2024 mechanical all-wheel drive. If you drive average miles, mostly on open roads, and the hybrid costs a lot more, the 4-cylinder can be the better deal.

## Choose the hybrid if

You drive a lot, especially in stop-and-go traffic, or you plan to keep the car for years. The 2018–2024 Hybrid LE is the efficiency champion of the used market, and from the 2020 model year its battery has a 10-year, 150,000-mile warranty. The 2025–2026 hybrid adds more power, a modern cabin and all-wheel drive on every grade. Run the math on the actual prices in front of you. A small hybrid premium pays off quickly, and a large one may never pay off.

## Choose the V6 if

You want the Camry that's actually quick. Car and Driver got the XSE V6 to 60 mph in 5.8 seconds and the TRD in 5.6, and the V6 is a smooth, strong engine that Toyota doesn't put in a Camry anymore. You'll pay more for fuel, and a clean, low-mile V6 often carries a premium. If that doesn't bother you, it's the most interesting car in this guide.

## Our pick depends on the driver

- **Choose the 4-cylinder** for the lowest price and the simplest ownership.
- **Choose the hybrid** for the lowest fuel bills and the smoothest city driving.
- **Choose the V6** because you like to drive, and you know what it costs.

Each choice can be the right one. None of them is right for everyone.

::: curiosity The Gas Camry That Out-Cruised a Hybrid
When the 2018 Camry arrived, its new base engine was so efficient that the 4-cylinder Camry L was rated at 41 mpg on the highway. That beat the outgoing 2017 Camry Hybrid SE and XLE, rated at 37 on the highway. In town, the hybrid still won easily. But for 1 model year, the cheapest gas Camry was the better highway cruiser.
:::
