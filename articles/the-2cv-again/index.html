The Original 2CV Motorized France. Can the Electric One Do It Again?

While America keeps arguing about how much range and power an EV needs, Citroën is asking a more basic question: how little car can ordinary people really live with?

By Jolice Franklyn · 8 minute read

The original Citroën 2CV was never meant to impress anyone at a charging station, a country club or a traffic light. It was designed to move people cheaply across roads that were often barely roads at all.

It was light, mechanically simple, slow and wonderfully useful. Its long-travel suspension shrugged off neglected rural surfaces, its roof rolled back, its seats came out, and its little air-cooled engine asked almost nothing of its owner. Its shape turned austerity into personality. Citroën built about 5.1 million 2CV cars and vans between 1949 and 1990, and somewhere along the way it stopped being transportation and became a symbol of practical freedom.

Now Citroën wants to bring that idea back as an affordable electric car. The 2CV Concept, unveiled on the evening of October 11, the eve of the Paris Motor Show, is a small EV, 3.76 m (12 ft 4 in) long, built around the original's lightness, accessibility and unmistakable character. Citroën says it previews a European-built production model that it will unveil within the next 2 years and that will join its range by 2028, with a target starting price around €15,000 (about $16,800). That price is the real headline.

The details are the most 2CV thing about it. The fabric roof rolls back by hand, the front bench comes out, the bumpers stand proud of the body so they can be replaced on their own, the door bins are designed to hold eggs, a nod to the original's famous brief, and the radio detaches and doubles as a speaker. It rides on 18-inch steel wheels. "This is the first time a revival has been shorter than the original version," said Citroën's design director, Pierre Leclercq.

The opposite of the American EV formula

Much of the American EV market was built from the top down. Automakers used big batteries, enormous power and expensive trucks and luxury crossovers to absorb the cost of new technology. That produced some astonishing vehicles, but it also made the electric car feel like a premium product. The average new vehicle sold in America in September cost $50,562, according to Kelley Blue Book, and the average EV $55,879. Even as the gap narrows, many buyers find an electric lineup dominated by crossovers carrying more battery, more weight and more performance than they need.

Europe's problem is different. Its cities are older, its streets narrower, its fuel more expensive and its daily drives generally shorter. A small EV with moderate range can be genuinely useful there without pretending to replace every vehicle for every trip. That lets Citroën offer a different bargain: less battery, less mass and less performance, in exchange for a price ordinary buyers might actually reach. The 2CV doesn't need to win a range contest. It needs to be easy to park, cheap to charge, comfortable in a dense city and able to finish routine trips without making its owner plan life around it.

"Sufficient" may be the next breakthrough

Range has become the electric car's version of horsepower: a number that's easy to market and hard to stop chasing. But every extra mile needs battery capacity, and capacity adds cost and weight. A smaller battery uses less raw material, charges in less time, wastes less energy carrying itself around and costs less to buy. It can also cut tire wear and the heavy, insulated feel of many bigger EVs.

The trade-off is obvious. A short-range 2CV would be a poor fit for long highway trips, for rural owners without dependable charging, or for a household that needs a single car to do everything. Affordable doesn't automatically mean practical for everyone. That's why the missing numbers matter. Citroën hasn't announced the battery size, the official range, the charging speed, the power or the final weight. It has given the concept a price and a philosophy before showing the arithmetic. Still, the philosophy may be exactly what the EV market needs.

The 2CV gives Citroën something no newcomer can manufacture quickly: memory.

Europe is turning heritage into a competitive weapon

Citroën isn't alone. Renault has brought back the 5 and the Twingo, Volkswagen is putting the Polo name on an affordable electric hatchback, and at the same Paris show Peugeot used the RCZ name to make future electric performance feel familiar. Renault's electric Twingo went on sale in France this year at €19,490 (about $21,800), and Volkswagen has 40,000 pre-orders for the ID. Polo, which starts at €24,995 (about $28,000), according to the advocacy group Transport & Environment.

This isn't just nostalgia. European automakers face Chinese competitors that can build attractive, well-equipped EVs at prices European factories struggle to match. BYD, Geely, Chery and Changan may not have a century of European history, but they have enormous advantages in batteries, supply chains and development speed. Citroën can't beat them by putting a different badge on another anonymous electric crossover. People already know what a 2CV stands for: modest without being joyless, unconventional without being threatening, inexpensive without pretending to be luxurious. If Citroën can translate those qualities, rather than just copying the old headlights, heritage becomes a real advantage.

Regulation will help decide whether the price is real

Citroën says the production car will comply with the EU's planned E-Car rules. The European Commission proposed a new category for small, affordable electric cars in December 2025: EVs up to 4.2 m (13 ft 9 in) long and made in the EU, which would earn automakers extra credit toward their CO2 targets and give governments and cities a single definition to aim incentives at. According to reporting on the proposal, each car would count as 1.3 vehicles toward those targets, and the category's requirements would be fixed for 10 years. None of it is law yet. A European Parliament committee vote planned for October 5 was pushed back, and a full vote is expected in November.

That's where the safety debate starts. The European Transport Safety Council, a road-safety group, says the proposal would effectively freeze safety requirements for these cars for up to 10 years. The case for the category is the other side of the same problem: when every small car has to carry much of the cost and complexity of a big SUV, the cheapest new car stops being cheap, and people keep older, less efficient and less safe cars for longer. The question for regulators, and for Citroën, is how much can be simplified without lowering protection.

The real test is whether simplicity survives production

Concept cars are easy places to celebrate simplicity. Production departments, regulations and option lists have a habit of adding weight and cost. Citroën has to resist turning the 2CV into a heavily equipped lifestyle object whose nostalgic shape hides an ordinary €25,000 (about $28,000) EV. It also has to give the car enough range, heating and crash protection to make ownership realistic rather than romantic.

If it succeeds, the electric 2CV could matter more than far faster and more expensive EVs, because it would show that electrification doesn't have to mean more technology, more power and more car. The original 2CV gave mobility to people who couldn't otherwise afford much of a car. Its successor will matter only if it does the same.

The short version

Citroën's 2CV Concept, revealed on October 11, is a 3.76 m (12 ft 4 in) electric car aiming for about 1,000 kg (2,205 lb) and a starting price around €15,000 (about $16,800), with a European-built production version due by 2028. It's the opposite of the American EV formula: less battery, less weight, less car. Citroën hasn't said how big the battery is, how far it goes or how it will hit the price, and the EU rules it's designed around aren't final yet.
