Park an old 4Runner next to a new one and they look as if they came back from the same camping trip. Underneath, they ask a buyer very different questions.

The fifth generation asks whether a proven, simple drivetrain is worth old-fashioned performance, an older cabin and a thirstier engine. The sixth asks whether more torque, a modern interior and better mileage are worth a higher price and a much shorter track record.

## The engines

|  | 2017–2024 V6 | 2025–2026 turbo | 2025–2026 hybrid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Engine | 4.0-liter V6 | 2.4-liter turbo 4-cylinder | 2.4-liter turbo 4-cylinder with an electric motor |
| Transmission | 5-speed automatic | 8-speed automatic | 8-speed automatic |
| Power | 270 hp | 278 hp | 326 hp |
| Torque | 278 lb-ft | 317 lb-ft | 465 lb-ft |
| EPA combined, 4WD | 17 mpg | 21 mpg | 23 mpg |
| Tows up to | 2,268 kg (5,000 lb) | 2,722 kg (6,000 lb) | 2,722 kg (6,000 lb) |
| Third row | Some versions | Available | Not offered |

The turbo's torque advantage matters far more in everyday driving than its 8 extra horsepower suggest, because it arrives so much lower in the rev range. The hybrid is a strong, specialized option, not an economy trim.

## The cost of driving

The EPA's combined ratings for four-wheel-drive versions make a fair comparison: 17 mpg for the V6, 21 for the turbo and 23 for the hybrid.

[[chart:mpg]]

At 15,000 miles a year and $4 a gallon, that works out to about $3,529, $2,857 and $2,609 a year. The gap between the V6 and the turbo is about $672 a year, and between the turbo and the hybrid about $248.

[[chart:fuelcost]]

These are examples, not forecasts. Your miles and your local gas price will move them, and insurance, tires, financing and resale value will move the total cost of ownership even more.

## What the newer truck actually improves

Modern safety and infotainment, an 8-speed automatic, a lot more torque, a stronger tow rating and a wider range of grades, from the luxurious Platinum to the overlanding Trailhunter. It keeps the body-on-frame toughness and the trail hardware. What it doesn't do is ride like a crossover. If comfort is the thing you disliked about the old 4Runner, the new one won't fix it.

## Why anyone would still buy the V6

Because a documented, well-kept V6 is a known quantity. Because a lower purchase price can outweigh several years of fuel savings. And because some drivers simply prefer a naturally aspirated engine and fewer systems to go wrong. Its reputation for durability is encouraging, but the condition of the truck in front of you is what decides it. Nothing about the badge cancels out rust, skipped maintenance or a badly installed lift kit.

## Our pick depends on the driver

- **Choose the V6** for the lowest price, simple and proven mechanicals, and rugged familiarity.
- **Choose the turbo** for everyday drivability and a modern cabin, provided the price works.
- **Choose the hybrid** for the torque, or for the grades that only come with it, not for Prius-style fuel bills.

Each choice can be the right one. None of them is right for everyone.

::: curiosity 22 Years of the Same V6
The 4.0-liter V6 under every 2017–2024 4Runner wasn't new even then. Toyota's 1GR-FE first went into an American-market vehicle in the 2003 4Runner and stayed through the 2024 model year: 22 model years in the same nameplate, the last 15 of them in this generation without any major changes. Along the way it also powered the Tacoma, the Tundra and the FJ Cruiser.
:::
