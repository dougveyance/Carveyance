Some vehicles are sold on a promise of adventure. The modern Ford Bronco comes with the practical inconveniences that make that promise believable. Its doors come off. Its roof comes off. Its floor, tires and suspension are designed around a different list of priorities than a school-run crossover's. You don't buy one because it's the quietest way across Los Angeles. You buy one because, once the pavement ends, a different kind of road begins.

## What Ford actually built

Ford revealed the Bronco on July 13, 2020, and the first production trucks left the Michigan Assembly Plant in Wayne on June 14, 2021, as 2021 models. The name had been off sale since 1996. It came back as a body-on-frame SUV with 2 or 4 doors, sharing its basic platform with the Ranger pickup but with its own body, its own suspension tuning and 4-wheel drive on every version. The 2-door seats 4 and the 4-door seats 5.

The front suspension is independent, which gives it more steering precision on pavement than a solid-axle rival, and the rear has a solid axle on 5 links and coil springs. The doors have frameless windows and can be stored in the cargo area, and the mirrors are mounted to the body, so they stay when the doors come off. With the Sasquatch Package's 35-inch tires, ground clearance is 11.6 inches.

## 2 engines, 2 gearboxes

The 2.3-liter turbo 4-cylinder is the sensible starting point, and it's no consolation prize. Ford rates it at 300 hp and 325 lb-ft on premium fuel. It's also the only engine offered with the 7-speed manual, which is really a 6-speed with an extra-low crawler gear, marked C on the shifter. That combination is compelling for drivers who value taking part more than a louder exhaust.

The 2.7-liter twin-turbo V6 makes 330 hp and 415 lb-ft on premium fuel and comes only with the 10-speed automatic. It's more effortless with passengers, hills and big tires. The Bronco Raptor, covered in the next chapter, has its own 418-hp 3.0-liter V6.

Car and Driver's tests give a fair picture of the spread. A 2.7-liter Wildtrak 4-door ran to 60 mph in 6.2 seconds, a 2.3-liter Outer Banks 4-door with the automatic in 6.5, a manual Black Diamond 2-door in 7.0, and a manual Badlands 4-door on 35-inch tires in 7.4. Big tires and a 4-door body cost more time than the engine choice saves.

## Fuel

[[chart:mpg]]

No Bronco is frugal. The EPA rates a 2026 2.3-liter with the automatic at 20 mpg combined and the manual at 19. A 2.7-liter Outer Banks is rated at 20, but most 2.7-liter Broncos come in at 17 or 18, and the Sasquatch Package's 35-inch tires drop any Bronco to 17 or 18. Choose the tires for the driving you'll really do.

## On the road, enthusiasm has a price

The Bronco is tall, blunt and fairly heavy. You hear its tires, feel the turbulence around the mirrors and notice how a short wheelbase can make broken pavement busy. The removable roof brings freedom and, compared with a fixed-roof SUV, more chances for wind noise and leaking seals. The steering is easy to live with, and the independent front suspension makes it less primitive than its silhouette suggests, but its priorities are off-road ones. Families looking at a 4-door should try the rear seat and the cargo area with their real child seats, strollers or weekend gear before buying.

## 2-door or 4-door

A 2-door turns into awkward spaces more easily and has the classic short-wheelbase proportions, but getting into the back seat takes effort and cargo room is limited. The 4-door is the practical answer for most families, and its longer wheelbase is calmer on long highway drives, even if the shorter truck has the edge on narrow trails. For 2026, the 2-door was offered only as a Base or a Badlands. Test both with the gear you actually carry, because photographs hide the trade-offs well.

## 4-wheel-drive hardware isn't all the same

Every Bronco has 4-wheel drive with a low range, but the axle ratios, transfer cases, locking differentials, tire sizes and the stabilizer-bar disconnect vary by trim and package. A Bronco on aggressive tires isn't necessarily a Badlands, and a Sasquatch truck has hardware changes beyond its rubber. Check the window sticker or the build sheet rather than guessing from its stance. The expensive off-road parts are worth having if you crawl over ledges, but they also bring costlier tires, more road noise and sometimes trouble fitting in a garage.

## The V6 deserves a measured discussion

The 2.7-liter V6 is the stronger engine, but more cylinders shouldn't become a blanket reliability ranking. Certain 2021–2022 Broncos with the 2.7-liter are covered by NHTSA recall 24V635, because the intake valves can break and cause engine failure and a loss of power. That's a defined recall population, not evidence that every V6 Bronco will fail. Get the VIN, check whether the dealer's engine test and any engine replacement were done, then judge the rest of the truck's condition separately.

## Manual charm, manual responsibilities

The manual 2.3-liter is unusually appealing in a modern off-roader, and the crawler gear is more than a naming gimmick, but inspect the gearbox as carefully as you would an automatic. Ford's service bulletin 23-2409 covers some 2021–2023 manual Broncos that lose 3rd gear or grind going into 3rd or 4th, and the repair replaces gears or synchronizers. On a fully warmed-up test drive, check every shift, and don't excuse persistent grinding as rugged character. A service bulletin isn't a recall, and it doesn't mean every truck is affected.

## The verdict

The Bronco can be a memorable only car, but the best one is sized, tired and equipped honestly. A modestly optioned 4-door on road-biased tires makes more sense for many owners than a wide, lifted specialty version, and a real off-roader may reasonably choose the opposite. Either way, the Bronco earns its place not by pretending the compromises don't exist but by giving its owner something worthwhile in exchange for them.
