The Toyota 86 exists because somebody at Toyota remembered that a sports car doesn't need enough horsepower to rearrange the weather. The recipe was almost stubbornly traditional when it arrived: engine in front, drive at the back, a low seat, a manual gearbox and an approachable price. It's still refreshingly clear in a market that treats acceleration as the whole of driving pleasure.

The appeal isn't that it can beat everything away from a traffic light. It can't. It's that the steering, the body's movements and the pedals tell you what's happening early enough to do something about it. On a winding road, the driver gets a vote. The engine sometimes sounds as if it's been asked to attend an event without refreshments, but the chassis makes up for a great deal.

## 2 engines and a big redesign

Through 2020, the US Toyota 86 used Subaru's 2.0-liter flat-4. Manual cars made 205 hp and 156 lb-ft, automatics 200 hp and 151 lb-ft. Its weakness was less the peak power than the midrange: maximum torque didn't arrive until 6,400 rpm, so the car felt flat until you wrung it out.

The GR86's 2.4-liter, new for 2022, makes 228 hp and 184 lb-ft, and its torque peaks at 3,700 rpm. That changed how the car leaves a corner, not just a number on the spec sheet.

The newer body and chassis are stronger, the cabin is more modern, and the experience is still thoroughly analog compared with most new cars. There are limits. The rear seats are best treated as occasional storage, it isn't quiet on the highway, and the automatic changes the car's rhythm considerably. The manual is the version that makes the most sense of what the car is for.

## What it feels like, and what it costs

The GR86 is lively without needing reckless speed. Turn-in is sharp, the balance is adjustable, and it rewards precise inputs. None of that replaces good judgment on a wet road. Rear-wheel drive and a modest weight mean tires, alignment and the driver's habits have an outsized effect on how it behaves. A well-kept stock car is a delight. A badly lowered, aggressively tuned one can feel like a different car altogether.

[[chart:zero60]]

Don't confuse a low purchase price with cheap neglect. Track days, hard cornering and performance tires are a normal part of this car's world. Ask how it was used, look at the tires and brakes, and ask for service records that match any hard driving. The GR86's engine has a mixed reputation among owners who track their cars. The checklist chapter explains what's known and what to ask, because forum stories about one engine say nothing about the car in front of you.

## Which one deserves your money?

If price comes first, a cared-for 2017–2020 86 manual is still a rewarding car, and often a smarter buy than a newer one with modifications and scuffs. If you can stretch, the GR86 is better exactly where the old car needed help: midrange pull and everyday usability. Neither is roomy or especially quiet. Both are alternatives to the Mazda MX-5, which offers an open roof and a different kind of involvement, and to the Subaru BRZ, which is mechanically almost the same car, with Subaru's own tuning.

The GR86 is honest in a way that can be uncomfortable for anyone expecting luxury-car quiet or hot-hatch acceleration. It's a machine designed to reward skill. That's still rare enough to celebrate.

::: curiosity 86 by 86
The 86 name points back to the AE86 Corolla of the 1980s, but the first car's engine made the joke on its own: its bore and stroke both measured exactly 86 mm. For the GR86, Subaru kept the 86 mm stroke and widened the bore to 94 mm to reach 2.4 liters, so only half of the pun survived the redesign.
:::
