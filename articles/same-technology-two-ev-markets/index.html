Same Technology, 2 Completely Different EV Markets

Electric-car sales are falling in America and setting records in Europe. The cars didn't suddenly get better on one side of the Atlantic. Everything around them did.

By Grayson Kollen · 7 minute read

Something odd is happening to the electric car. In the United States, EV sales fell 30.7% in the first 9 months of 2026, and EVs were about 6% of new-vehicle sales, down from 8.5% a year earlier. In Europe, the same months went the other way: EVs took 23.2% of the market, up from 17.7%. The same basic technology is being turned down by one set of buyers and embraced by another, at the same time.

It's often explained as culture: Americans dislike EVs, Europeans have accepted them. The real explanation is more practical. Europe and America have built 2 different markets around the same machine.

America removed the incentive before EVs got cheap

The federal tax credit of up to $7,500 hid one of the American EV market's central weaknesses: many EVs were still expensive. When it ended on September 30, 2025, buyers didn't just lose a government benefit. The effective price of many EVs went up overnight. Automakers and leasing companies had used the credit to build cheap monthly payments, and some of those leases went away with it, so the drop that followed was predictable. Ford's EV sales fell 68% through September and GM's 43%. Honda canceled 3 EVs it had planned to build in the US and will stop selling the Prologue, its only US EV, after 2026.

Tesla and Rivian held up better than the market. Tesla's US sales fell an estimated 14%, and Rivian's rose 29%, helped by the cheaper R2. Buyers will still choose an EV that has a clear identity, and the established automakers appear to have a bigger problem than the companies built around electric cars.

America electrified its biggest vehicles first

The American product mix made electrification unusually expensive. Instead of starting with small hatchbacks and city cars, automakers put enormous batteries into pickups, large SUVs and luxury crossovers, vehicles that need a lot of energy to move a lot of weight, cover long distances and keep the performance Americans expect. The results were impressive, and costly. Electric pickups also show a battery's limits more plainly than anything else on the road. Towing cuts range sharply, and charging with a trailer attached is still awkward at many stations, while a gasoline or diesel truck can add hundreds of miles in a few minutes. That doesn't make electric pickups useless. It makes them a hard product with which to win over a skeptical mass market.

Europe got smaller, cheaper EVs

Europe took a different path. Almost 40 new mass-market electric models went on sale there in the first half of 2026, taking the total past 150, and sales of EVs priced under €25,000 (about $28,000) are on course to be about 7 times their 2024 level this year, according to Transport & Environment, a Brussels-based advocacy group. Volkswagen's ID. Polo sold out, with more than 40,000 orders and a 10-month wait, T&E said. Cars like the Renault 5 and Citroën ë-C3 fit the part of the market where electric driving makes the most intuitive sense: small cars, short daily distances and expensive fuel.

Europe's tightening fleet CO2 targets gave automakers a strong reason to build and sell those cars, national incentives cut purchase and lease costs in some countries, and high fuel prices made the running-cost case. Many European drivers don't need a big SUV or a pickup, and a smaller battery gives useful range when it's moving less car. Less battery means less weight and less cost, and lower cost brings more buyers in.

America didn't reject electrification. It moved toward the versions that ask the least money and the least change.

China is part of Europe's advantage, and its dilemma

Europe also allows something America largely doesn't: direct competition from Chinese EV makers. BYD, MG, Leapmotor and others widened the choice and pushed European brands to cut prices and move faster. In the US, tariffs and the connected-vehicle rule keep most Chinese-built cars out, which shields American automakers and, in Washington's view, addresses data-security risks, but it also keeps out many of the cheap EVs that have grown Europe's market. Europe is now dealing with the other side of that choice. Chinese cars help make electrification affordable, but their growth worries European automakers and their workers, and the EU has put duties on Chinese EVs and is now capping Chinese hybrids. Europe wants the inexpensive cars without losing the industry that builds them, which may not be possible unless more of those cars are built in Europe.

Hybrids are taking American demand

American buyers haven't given up on efficiency. They've chosen a different version of it. Hybrid sales rose 23% in the first 3 quarters of 2026, to about 15.6% of the market, and used-EV sales rose 19%, according to CarGurus. Those numbers say 2 things: buyers still want relief from fuel costs, and they remain very sensitive to price and inconvenience. A conventional hybrid needs no change in habits and usually costs less than a comparable long-range EV, and a used EV offers electric driving after the first owner has absorbed much of the depreciation.

Chargers are growing faster than confidence

The US charging network kept growing even as new-EV sales fell. By June, the Department of Energy counted more than 250,000 public charging ports, including nearly 74,000 fast-charging ports. But reliability and location matter as much as the count. A charger that's broken, slow or blocked doesn't build confidence, and drivers remember one failed stop far longer than dozens of good ones. Home charging is still an EV's biggest advantage, but only for people with a reliable place to park and plug in; apartment dwellers and street parkers live with a very different car. Europe hasn't solved every version of that problem either. Charging, incentives and EV adoption vary enormously from country to country, and Norway and the Netherlands aren't the whole continent.

EV demand is built, not found

The Atlantic divide shows that EV adoption isn't simply a vote on whether buyers like electric motors. Governments shape the effective price. Regulations decide what automakers must build. Trade policy decides who gets to compete. Fuel prices set running costs, housing decides whether home charging is possible, and vehicle size decides how much battery a car needs. Change those conditions and buyers change with them. Europe built a market with expensive fuel, stricter emissions rules, more affordable models and more competition. America removed its main purchase incentive, loosened its fuel-economy rules, kept out cheap Chinese entrants and concentrated on expensive trucks and SUVs. The result shouldn't surprise anyone.

Europe didn't prove that every driver wants an EV, and America didn't prove that the electric car has failed. Together they proved that technology doesn't succeed on its own. The car matters, and so does the world built around it.

The short version

EVs were 6% of US new-vehicle sales through September and 23.2% in Europe. America lost its $7,500 credit while most of its EVs were still big and expensive; Europe got more than 150 models, many under €25,000, along with strict CO2 targets, expensive fuel and Chinese competition. American buyers still wanted efficiency and bought hybrids and used EVs instead.
