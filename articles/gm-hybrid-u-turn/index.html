GM Spent Years Saying No to Hybrids. Now It Says "We Will Have Hybrids."

The company that built a hybrid Tahoe and the Chevrolet Volt ended up with a single hybrid, a Corvette. Its dealers asked, its market share slipped and Toyota kept selling. A hybrid Traverse would be GM's most ordinary product decision in years, and maybe its most overdue.

By Jolice Franklyn · 7 minute read

On October 20, an analyst asked Mary Barra about hybrids, and the chief executive of General Motors gave the shortest answer GM has given on the subject in a decade. "We will have hybrids. They have already been added."

A week later, GM said it would prepare its Lansing Delta Township plant in Michigan, which builds the Chevrolet Traverse, GMC Acadia and Buick Enclave, for hybrid production, using a federal grant it had originally won to prepare a different Lansing plant for EVs. GM didn't name the models. People familiar with the plans told Reuters they'll be hybrid versions of those 3 SUVs. "It is not going to be additional CapEx and engineering, and we will talk more about them as we get closer to the launch," Barra said. John Murphy, founder of the advisory firm The MAP and a former BofA auto analyst, called it "a good step in the right direction," and added: "You don't typically get a major powertrain decision mid-cycle."

GM was early, and then it left

None of this is new technology for GM. In 2008 it put a hybrid Tahoe and Yukon on sale, using a Two-Mode system it developed with DaimlerChrysler and BMW, then added the Escalade, Silverado and Sierra. They were clever and expensive and didn't sell: at their peak, in 2009, the Two-Mode hybrids found 8,797 buyers across 5 models, and by 2011 it was 3,114, Green Car Reports counted. A 2013 Silverado Hybrid started at $40,885, about $59,100 in today's money, for an EPA rating of 21 mpg combined. In 2012, GM canceled the next generation. "So with this next generation it will not be a hybrid," said Mark Reuss, then head of GM North America.

The Chevrolet Volt, a plug-in hybrid that drove its first miles on electricity, went on sale in December 2010 and ended in 2019. That January, as he became GM's president, Reuss said "Hybrids are just countermeasures to an ICE," and Green Car Reports ran the headline "GM president dashes hopes of future Volt, says no more hybrids." The plan was to go straight to electric.

Buyers went the other way

While GM waited for electric, Americans bought hybrids. They reached a record 16.3% of US sales in the second quarter of 2026, according to Cox Automotive, against about 5.8% for EVs, and J.D. Power and GlobalData forecast 18.6% of retail sales for October. GM's only hybrid in America today is the Corvette E-Ray. Its share of the US market fell to 16.6% in the first 9 months of the year, from 17.4%, while Toyota and Honda gained, and dealers told Reuters they'd been asking for hybrids "every chance we get."

Barra has argued that the hybrid boom is mostly in segments GM doesn't sell in. "A lot of where the hybrid activity is, it is in compact car, midsize car, and minivans," she said on the call, and the Equinox, its compact SUV, "is fully maxed out even without a hybrid." That's fair as far as it goes. But the 3-row family SUV is exactly where GM does compete, and where its rivals have been selling hybrids for years.

What GM still has to show

Almost everything that matters to a buyer is still unknown: the models, the system, the fuel economy, the price and the date. Murphy expects the hybrids possibly late next year, and GM hasn't said. Barra's line about no additional engineering suggests GM already has the hardware it needs, but it hasn't said what that hardware is. GM is also still promising plug-in hybrids, and its long-term goal hasn't changed. "Our long-term vision is an all-electric future," Mike Anderson, GM's head of propulsion engineering, said in October. "We're not tone deaf to our customers. We know what they want and we want to give that to them as quickly as we can."

That's the right instinct, and it arrives late. GM's pickups are paying for its electric and self-driving future, and they'll keep doing it. But the families shopping for a Traverse this year have been driving to the Toyota store for a Grand Highlander Hybrid, and every one of them who leaves happy is a customer GM has to win back. A hybrid Traverse won't make headlines the way eyes-off driving or a new battery chemistry does. It's the kind of car that keeps a company in business while it builds the future, and GM spent a decade acting as if it didn't need one.

The short version

GM built hybrid Tahoes in 2008 and the Volt in 2010, then dropped both and bet on going straight to electric. While it waited, hybrids grew to about 1 in 6 US sales, Toyota and Honda gained share, and GM's only hybrid became the Corvette E-Ray. Now Barra says "we will have hybrids," and GM is preparing the Lansing plant that builds the Traverse, Acadia and Enclave. GM hasn't given models, fuel economy, prices or a date.
