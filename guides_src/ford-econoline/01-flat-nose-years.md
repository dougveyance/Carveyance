The 1961 original was pure economy-of-means: a 90-inch-wheelbase unibody using the Falcon's 85-hp 144 six, mounted between the seats and driving the rear wheels. That layout meant a flat load floor and 204 cubic feet for $1,881 — about $21,200 in 2026 dollars — and it buried the Corvair Greenbrier and Dodge A100 on the sales charts, 552,004 built in 7 model years. The range ran from 8-door cargo vans to the Falcon-badged Station Bus and Club Wagon to the cab-over Econoline pickup, and the one thing it could never have was a V8; there was simply nowhere to put it.

The 1969 second generation fixed that by pushing the engine ahead of the axle on a 15-inch-longer wheelbase with F-Series suspension underneath — 240 and 300 sixes, the 302 V8 at last, and Club Wagon, Deluxe and Chateau trims with real family aspirations. Then came the quietly historic 1972 Hi-Cube: the first cutaway chassis aimed at upfitters, which turned the Econoline from a vehicle into an industry and set up the motorhome boom of the decade.

## Buying one now

Flat-nose first generations are honest collectibles: Falcon mechanicals mean parts are a catalog away, and the pickup is the blue-chip of the family — clean ones have brought mid-5-figures at auction while vans and wagons trail well behind. Check the front floor and cab corners for rot, the mid-engine doghouse for heat-cooked wiring, and accept that 85 hp means planning your freeway merges a ZIP code ahead. Second generations are rarer in good shape than you'd think, because the custom-van craze consumed them; a straight, uncut 1969–74 shell is the find, and a tastefully surviving shag-and-porthole custom is period art — buy it as-is or not at all.

Either way, these are now show-field vans more than tools. Rust decides everything, originality is climbing, and the money is still a fraction of what equivalent-condition pickups bring — which is exactly why the smart collectors are quietly moving in.
