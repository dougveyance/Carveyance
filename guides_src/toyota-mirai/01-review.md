The Mirai solves range anxiety in a way that makes infrastructure anxiety impossible to ignore. Instead of a big battery you plug in, it carries compressed hydrogen, feeds it to a fuel cell stack and uses the resulting electricity to drive an electric motor. Water is the main thing that comes out of the tailpipe. It's an elegant answer to a question most American drivers never asked, but one worth understanding.

Behind the technology is a perfectly recognizable car. The second-generation Mirai, introduced for 2021, traded its awkward predecessor's shape for a low, stylish rear-drive sedan. Quiet power, a relaxed chassis and a comfortable cabin make it pleasant in all the ordinary ways. On the highway, the refinement is easier to appreciate than the chemistry under the floor.

## 2 generations, 2 identities

The first generation, sold here through the 2020 model year, looked like a concept car that had been allowed to keep its shipping container. Its styling advertised the technology more loudly than many owners would have liked. It drove the front wheels with 151 hp, seated 4 and was rated at 312 miles.

The second generation brought a longer, sleeker body, rear-wheel drive, a fifth seat and the road presence of a premium sedan. Toyota rates the 2026 Mirai at 182 hp and 402 miles. Like any range figure, that's a test result, not a promise at every temperature, speed and road.

Hydrogen storage, fuel cell controls, high-voltage electrics and specialized service procedures make the Mirai much more specialized than a Toyota hybrid. That isn't proof that it's unreliable. It's a reason to take dealer access, parts and the warranty seriously. Toyota covers the key fuel cell parts for 8 years or 100,000 miles.

## The hydrogen elephant in the room

A car can go 400 miles between fill-ups and still be a hassle if the nearest working pump is far away. Nearly all of the country's retail hydrogen stations are in California, and there are only a few dozen of them. The network has broken down more than once. Shell closed its California car stations in 2024. Then, on February 24, 2026, a hydrogen trailer exploded at a truck yard in Colton, killing a technician, and deliveries of compressed hydrogen were paused while it was investigated. By late March, 32 of California's 52 stations were closed, and at the worst point only 9 were available statewide. Most came back that spring, but drivers spent weeks in long lines at the few that stayed open.

Price is the other half. True Zero, the state's largest station operator, charged $13.14 a kilogram in 2021 (about $16 in 2026 money). It has since charged $36. The US Department of Energy's April 2026 report put the national average at $28.46.

So treat fueling as a core part of the car, not a footnote under the horsepower. Before buying, check the stations you'd actually use, whether they reliably have fuel at the times you'd go, and what they charge. Read the terms of any fuel card you're offered, including whether it stays with the car if you sell it. A low price on a used Mirai can look very tempting, partly because the fueling situation shrinks the number of people it makes sense for.

## A sedan beneath the technology

The Mirai's electric drive is smooth and quiet. The second-generation cabin feels like a deliberate attempt to make the technology less intimidating, with conventional screens and controls, nice materials and a refined ride. But the packaging isn't as generous as the shape suggests. The hydrogen tanks take up space, the rear seat is tighter than the long body implies, and the 9.6 cubic-foot trunk is small for a big sedan.

A used Lexus ES Hybrid or Toyota Crown is less unusual, and neither depends on a small hydrogen network. A battery EV you can charge at home can also be easier to live with, even if its rated range is lower. The Mirai's strengths only show up when its owner has dependable fuel.

## Who should, and shouldn't, buy one

The right Mirai owner lives close to several dependable hydrogen stations, is comfortable relying on a specialized dealer, and knows exactly what running one costs. The wrong buyer is the one tempted by a surprisingly low price who assumes filling it is like stopping at a gas station.

The Mirai is one of Toyota's most ambitious road cars of the past decade. It's also proof of a hard truth: a brilliant vehicle isn't automatically a practical product. Good engineering can't repeal geography.

::: curiosity A Name Full of Hope
Mirai means "future" in Japanese. Toyota gave an optimistic name to a car whose fortunes depend on a very old-fashioned question: does the nearest station have any fuel today?
:::
